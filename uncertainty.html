Rigor · uncertainty propagation

How robust is that?

The hardest-cases scorecard uses hard cutoffs, and pass/fail hides a metal that sits just under a cutoff. This page puts an uncertainty on every input and runs the scorecard 20,000 times. The pass/fail line becomes a probability, and the “only gallium and germanium” headline is shown with its uncertainty.

The uncertainty model and its main assumption

For each material, each axis input is drawn from a distribution and the five failure flags are recomputed, 20,000 times: companionality ~ N(mean, 12pp); top-producer share ~ N(mean, 8pp) (the cross-source spread measured at 6.4pp on this data); recycling ~ N(mean, 5pp); demand growth ~ mean×exp(N(0, 0.40)), because forward demand depends strongly on the scenario; world tonnage ~ mean ±~10%. The page reports, per material, the probability that each axis fails, the expected number of failures with a 90% interval, and P(hardest) = P(≥4 of 5 fail).

Limits. The five spreads are themselves judgement calls. A wider demand sigma pulls more metals into contention; a tighter one sharpens the top. The aim is not a precise probability. It is the shape of the ranking, and which results survive perturbation. Deterministic (fixed seed). Inputs: the five layer JSONs → uncertainty.json.

P(hardest) — probability a material fails 4 or 5 of the 5 axes

A gradient rather than a cut-off. The bracket marks the 90% interval on the number of axes failed. Two materials are almost certain. A cluster behind them is borderline, which the binary scorecard did not show.

The objection this page could not answer — so we tested it

“A Monte-Carlo propagates input uncertainty. It cannot validate the model.” This objection is correct. Everything above draws the five inputs, but the design was held fixed: the five axes are chosen rather than derived, the thresholds (66, 60, 10, 2.5, 50 kt) are constants that were never drawn, and “≥4 of 5” is a constant too. So “gallium 99%” only ever meant “99% of draws, under this design”. Read as the probability that gallium is the hardest case, it would turn a designer’s choices into false precision.

The design was therefore perturbed as well. The thresholds were drawn instead of fixed and moved ±15%, the rule was changed to 3-of-5 and 5-of-5, and each axis was dropped in turn. In the ablations the bar stays at “fails all but one”, so each one tests the axis and does not bring in a stricter rule. Eleven specifications. The results point in opposite directions, and both are shown.

The axes are not independent

The scorecard counts five axes as if each were a separate way to be stuck. They are not.
