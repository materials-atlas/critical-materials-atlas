Steel alloying metals · Research note

Niobium has one main source; for steel's other alloying metals, exposure depends on the buyer

Brazil mined 93% of the world's niobium in 2024 and supplied most of every large importer's ferroniobium. Indonesia shipped 93% of traded ferronickel, almost all of it to China, and none of what the United States imported.

Evidence review · 25 November 2026 · Claim register

Steel is iron with a little carbon and additions of other metals. Chromium makes it stainless, nickel and molybdenum improve its resistance to corrosion and heat, and manganese, niobium and vanadium add strength. This note sets out where six of these metals are mined, where the alloys are made, and where six large importing markets buy them. It then reports what the sources say about how much is used, what can replace it, and how concentrated the main inputs of steel itself are.

Finding. A world share does not tell an importer its own exposure. In 2024 Indonesia shipped 93% of traded ferronickel. China took 91% of that trade, and Indonesia supplied 97% of China's imports, 40% of the EU's, none of the United States' and almost none of Japan's. Niobium is the clearest case of a single source among the six metals. Brazil mined 93% of it in 2024, and in that year it was the largest supplier of ferroniobium to each of the six markets, with between 54% and 98% of their imports. USGS, niobium, 2026 edition. Three patterns appear. Ferroniobium has one pattern for everyone: Brazil was the largest supplier of all six markets in 2024. South Africa comes close for two other products, as the largest supplier of five of the six markets for both ferrochromium and manganese ore. Ferronickel has the highest world share in the table and the widest spread between buyers, from none to 97%. For ferrovanadium and ferromolybdenum the leading exporter has 21% and 28% of trade. CEPII BACI V202601.

Trade shares include re-exports. The Netherlands, China, Malaysia and Singapore, which mine little or no niobium, together appear with 16% of recorded ferroniobium exports, and that tonnage is counted a second time in the world total. India shows the effect: its imports were 3,859 tonnes in 2024, 34% of them through Singapore and Malaysia, and in 2019 Singapore was its largest recorded supplier with 61%.

The tables above count tonnes of alloy. Alloys from different countries do not carry the same amount of metal, so a share of tonnage is not a share of metal. US customs records both the gross weight and the contained metal of ferroalloy imports, by country of origin, for every alloy here except ferroniobium. This section takes the content of US imports from each origin in 2024, or in the nearest year from 2022 to 2025 where the 2024 flow was under 1,000 tonnes, and applies it to everything that origin exported in 2024. The conversion changes the picture for two alloys. South African ferrochromium is the lower-grade kind: US imports from South Africa contained 49% chromium in 2024, against 69% for imports from Kazakhstan. Both figures sit inside the ranges the industry association gives for the two kinds of alloy, 45% to 56% and 60% to 70%. On these contents South Africa's 53% of ferrochromium tonnage is about 46% to 47% of the chromium, and Kazakhstan's 22% of tonnage is about 27%. The US sample is small beside what these countries export, 4% of South Africa's tonnage and 2% of Kazakhstan's, so the result assumes they ship the same grade to other buyers. International Chromium Development Association, ferrochrome.

For ferronickel the Indonesian product is mostly nickel pig iron. USGS estimates of Indonesia's output imply a nickel content of about 14.6%, and one producer reports 12.2% for its own nickel pig iron. Imports from other origins measured 21% to 40%. On the two Indonesian figures, Indonesia's 93% of tonnage is about 85% to 89% of the nickel. Neither figure is a customs measurement, because the United States imports no Indonesian ferronickel, and neither is a lower limit: at 10% nickel Indonesia's share would be about 84%. USGS Minerals Yearbook 2024, Indonesia. Nickel Industries, Annual Report 2024.

For the other alloys the conversion settles less. The leading shares of ferromanganese and ferromolybdenum barely move: India has 35% of the tonnage and 35% to 36% of the manganese, and South Korea 28% of the tonnage and 27% to 28% of the molybdenum. For ferroniobium the conversion cannot change a share, because one assumed content is applied to every origin. For ferrovanadium the content of most traded tonnage is unknown and the leading exporter's share could be anywhere from 21% to 31%.

Contained metal also allows a rough comparison with mining. The ferronickel traded in 2024 carried about 1.3 to 1.6 million tonnes of nickel, about 35% to 42% of the nickel mined that year. Ferrovanadium in trade carried 18 to 27 thousand tonnes of vanadium, 16% to 23% of output, so most vanadium does not cross a border as ferrovanadium. Manganese ore in trade carried roughly 79% to 88% of the manganese mined. That figure is rough because the sources conflict: at USGS grades, South Africa's recorded ore exports carry 1.2 times the manganese that USGS has it mining. Ore grade shifts shares there too: Gabon's higher-grade ore is 18% of traded tonnage and 22% of the manganese, and Ghana's lower-grade ore is 11% of tonnage and 8%. Ferroniobium in trade carried about 91 thousand tonnes of niobium at the 65% USGS assumes, or 84 to 99 thousand at the 60% to 70% it gives as the range, a figure inflated by the re-exports described above. USGS Minerals Yearbook 2023, manganese. USGS Professional Paper 1802-M.

Limits. The content of an origin's exports to the United States is assumed to hold for its exports to everyone. Contents are for 2024 where the flow was large enough and otherwise for 2022, 2023 or 2025, and the manganese ore grades are for 2023. US customs records no content for ferroniobium. Ferrovanadium is sold in several grades, from about 40% to 80% vanadium, and unmeasured origins take 45% to 80%. About half of traded ferromolybdenum comes from origins with no measurement. Chromium ore is not converted, because no reliable grade by origin was found. A record more than 15 points from the middle value for its code is treated as a reporting anomaly and left out.

Niobium: two companies in Brazil and one in Canada

USGS puts Brazil's share of 2024 mine output at 93%. In its November 2026 outlook it writes: “Two companies produce these materials in Brazil, and one company produces them in Canada.” It estimates world mine capacity in 2025 at 126,000 tonnes a year, 92% of it in Brazil and 6% in Canada. So the old description of niobium as one mine was not accurate: there are two main producing companies in Brazil, and USGS records a third that made 4,008 tonnes of niobium and tantalum alloys in 2022. One of the two is much the larger. It reports sales of 100,173 tonnes of ferroniobium equivalent in 2025 and a capacity of 150,000 tonnes a year in the same unit, and writes that “we maintain a production capacity (150,000 tonnes/ year) that exceeds global consumption”. The other reports 10,348 tonnes of niobium in 2025. Ferroniobium is about 65% niobium, so the company figures and the USGS figures, which are in tonnes of niobium, are in different units, and no source read gives the companies' shares on one basis. The level of world output is also less firm than the share: the USGS yearbook for 2022 gives 83,700 tonnes of niobium worldwide and 75,600 in Brazil, well below its annual summaries. Both series put Brazil at about nine-tenths. USGS World Minerals Outlook, November 2026. USGS Minerals Yearbook 2022, niobium. CBMM, 2025 Sustainability Report. CMOC, 2025 Annual Report.

Brazil's share of recorded ferroniobium trade, 72% in 2024 and 79% in 2019, is lower than its share of mining. One reason is the re-exports described above. China took 30% of recorded ferroniobium trade in 2024 and bought 96% of it from Brazil. The United States bought 62% from Brazil and the rest almost wholly from Canada. CEPII BACI V202601.

Chromium: the ore is mined in one place and the alloy made in another

South Africa mined 46% of the world's chromium ore in 2024 and shipped 83% of the ore that was traded. China took 56% of traded ore and smelts it into ferrochromium. USGS estimates that China made 49% of the world's ferrochromium in 2023.

The sources do not agree on every figure. South Africa's ore exports in the trade record for 2024, 24.8 million tonnes, are larger than the USGS figure for its mine output, 22.9 million. The industry association's regional figures for 2025 put South Africa at 63% of about 40 million tonnes of ore, a higher share than the USGS one. On policy, the OECD writes that “chromium ore exports from both South Africa and Zimbabwe are affected by some form of export restrictions” and that “These two countries together account for about 88% of global chromium ore exports.” USGS, in February 2026, speaks of “Potential export controls and tariffs on chromite ore from South Africa may also affect ferrochromium production in China.” The OECD also writes: “Around 90% to 95% of mined chrome ore is converted into ferrochrome, and the stainless-steel sector accounts for 80% to 90% of global ferrochrome use”. USGS writes: “Chromium has no substitute in stainless steel, the leading end use, or in superalloys, the major strategic end use.” Stainless steel is defined by its chromium: “Stainless steels are corrosion resistant steels with a minimum of 10.5 per cent of chromium (Cr).” OECD Steel Outlook 2026. USGS, chromium, 2026 edition. worldstainless.

Vanadium: a co-product of steelmaking

China produced 71% of the world's vanadium in 2024 on the USGS figures, which leave out secondary supply. Most vanadium is not mined for itself. USGS writes: “In 2025, China continued to be the world's top vanadium producer, with most of its production originating as a coproduct from vanadiferous titanomagnetite ores processed during steelmaking.” The industry association puts steelmaking slag at about 59% of world vanadium supply, mines worked for vanadium at about 26% and secondary sources at about 15%. Supply therefore follows the output of particular steelworks. In ferrovanadium trade Austria (21%) and Czechia (15%), which mine no vanadium, are first and third, and China (20%) is second. In the United States, “Metallurgical use, primarily as an alloying agent for iron and steel, accounted for more than 90% of domestic reported vanadium consumption in 2025.” No world figure for the share of vanadium used in steel was found. USGS, vanadium, 2026 edition. Vanitec.

Nickel: Indonesia's alloy goes to China

Indonesia mined 62% of the world's nickel in 2024. The OECD writes that “since 2020, Indonesia has imposed a full ban on nickel ore exports” and that “Indonesia's share of global refined nickel output rose from 6% in 2015 to 61% in 2024”, citing a newspaper for the second figure. The trade record shows what followed. World trade under the ferronickel code rose from 2.8 million tonnes in 2019 to 10.0 million in 2024, by weight of alloy. Indonesia's share of it went from 56% to 93%, and China took 91% of the total. The other five markets bought much smaller tonnages. India and South Korea also bought mainly from Indonesia, the EU bought 40% of its imports there, and the United States and Japan almost nothing. USGS, nickel, 2026 edition. OECD Steel Outlook 2026. CEPII BACI V202601.

Stainless steel took 66% of nickel in first use in 2024 and batteries 16%, on the industry association's figures. USGS gives a figure for the United States only: “Stainless and alloy steel and nickel-containing alloys typically account for more than 85% of domestic consumption.” Nickel Institute, 2025. USGS, nickel, 2026 edition.

How much is used, and what can replace it

World and US figures differ. For niobium, USGS gives the US split: “Major end-use distribution of domestic niobium consumption was estimated as follows: steels, about 77%, and superalloys, about 23%.” For the world it reports, from market consultancies, that “Steel, primarily HSLA steel used in structural, automotive, and aerospace applications, accounted for approximately 89% of global niobium consumption” in 2022. The previous version of this page gave the US figure as if it were the world's. USGS, niobium, 2026 edition. USGS Minerals Yearbook 2022, niobium.

The quantities differ by a factor of several hundred. Stainless steel is at least a tenth chromium. Niobium in structural steel is measured in hundredths of a per cent: a review of microalloyed steels gives 0.02% to 0.06% as the usual range, or 200 to 600 grams per tonne of steel. The world steel association counts the grades: “There are more than 3,500 different grades of steel with many different physical, chemical, and environmental properties.” Baker (2015). World Steel Association.

Substitution is possible for some metals and not for others. For chromium in stainless steel and for manganese USGS states that there is none: “Manganese has no satisfactory substitute in its major applications.” For molybdenum: “There is little substitution for molybdenum in its major application in steels and cast irons.” Niobium and vanadium can partly replace each other. USGS lists “molybdenum and vanadium as alloying elements in high-strength low-alloy steels” among substitutes for niobium and warns that “a performance loss or higher cost may ensue”. The standard for one common structural steel leaves the choice to the steelmaker: “The use of columbium (niobium), vanadium, titanium, nitrogen, or combinations thereof, within the limitations noted in Section 5, is required; the selection of type (1, 2, 3, or 5) is at the option of the producer, unless otherwise specified by the purchaser.” The change is not quick. A USGS paper on vanadium says: “Replacement of vanadium with other mineral commodities requires significant technical adjustments to the steel production process to ensure that product specifications and quality are not compromised.” It adds that “substitution for vanadium is normally not considered for short-term changes in market conditions because of the considerable effort involved in implementing the change”. USGS, manganese, 2026 edition. USGS, molybdenum, 2026 edition. USGS, niobium, 2026 edition. ASTM A572/A572M. USGS Professional Paper 1802-U.

USGS gives recycling shares for the United States only. In 2025 recycled chromium was 21% of apparent consumption there and nickel from scrap about 60%. Of the niobium recycled, USGS writes that “it may have been as much as 20% of apparent consumption”, and of molybdenum that the amount “may be as much as 30% of the apparent supply of molybdenum”. These metals are recovered inside steel scrap and not as separate metals. USGS, chromium, 2026 edition. USGS, nickel, 2026 edition. USGS, niobium, 2026 edition. USGS, molybdenum, 2026 edition.

The main inputs of steel are concentrated in trade as well

The previous version of this page said that iron ore and coking coal “are everywhere”. Iron ore is mined in many countries: Australia had 38% of 2024 output and Brazil 16%. Trade is narrower. In 2025 Australia shipped 53% of the iron ore that crossed a border and Brazil 24%, together 77%, and China took 75% of imports. For coking coal, Canadian government figures give Australia 151 of the 363 million tonnes exported in 2024, or 42%, followed by Russia (56), the United States (52) and Mongolia (48). The Australian government's own table for the same year differs: a world total of 350 million tonnes, with Mongolia second at 57 and Russia fourth at 37. For scrap, the OECD reports that “only about 15.2% of global scrap trade was actually subject to restrictions”. USGS, iron ore, 2026 edition. Australian Government, Resources and Energy Quarterly, September 2026. Natural Resources Canada, Coal facts. OECD Steel Outlook 2026.

What the customs records cover

Customs records ferroalloys by gross weight, and their metal content varies. Ferroniobium is about 65% niobium. Ferrovanadium is sold in grades from about 40% to 80% vanadium. The ferronickel code is headed “Ferro-nickel”. Under the chapter's definition an iron alloy with more than 10% of alloying elements is a ferro-alloy, so nickel pig iron above that grade is recorded there and lower grades count as pig iron. The tonnage under the code is therefore far larger than the nickel it carries. Two things cannot be separated at this level of the classification: ores of niobium, tantalum and vanadium share one code, and coking coal shares a code with other bituminous coal. What remains open

Mine shares and trade shares are measured. Several things are not. The share of each company in niobium supply is not published on one basis. No world figure was found for the share of vanadium, manganese or molybdenum that goes into steel. One figure quoted in an earlier review, China at 51% of ferrochromium in 2024, could not be checked because the document would not open, and it is not used here. Stocks of ferroalloys held by steelmakers and traders are not published, so this note says nothing about how long an interruption could be absorbed. It makes no estimate of the effect of an interruption on steel output. The metal content of Indonesian ferronickel exports, of ferroniobium, of most traded ferrovanadium and of about half of traded ferromolybdenum is not measured.

Revision note · 25 November 2026

The previous overview was headed “Steel is abundant; the metals that make it strong are not”. It described niobium as coming from essentially one Brazilian mine and one company, gave 77% as the share of niobium used in steel, which is the US figure, showed China at 60% of ferrochromium without a source, left out nickel, and said iron ore and coking coal were everywhere. This revision uses the published tables for six metals, adds trade by weight for six importing markets, and separates world figures from US ones. The claim register lists each change.

Later the same day a section was added that restates the trade in contained metal. It uses the content of each origin's alloys as measured in US import records where such records exist, and USGS figures elsewhere.
