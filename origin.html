Method · origin tracing

What's hidden behind the refiner

The origin gap, traced — as a first-order estimate. Each country's imports are re-attributed from the apparent supplier (often a refiner or hub) to a likely mine origin, showing how much of a nation's critical-material supply is sourced via a refiner rather than the producer its customs data names.

Each country’s imports are re-attributed from the refiner or hub that shipped them back to the mine that likely produced the ore — showing how much of its supply really comes via a middleman.
The rule, and why it’s an upper bound

For every import flow: if the supplier genuinely mines the material (world mine share ≥ 5%) the origin stays the supplier; otherwise the supplier is treated as a refiner or hub, and the value is re-attributed to the material’s dominant mine. A country’s hidden dependence is the share of its imports re-attributed away from the apparent supplier.

First-order trace — an upper bound. We don’t observe each refiner’s ore sourcing, so all refiner-fronted flow is assigned to the single leading producer; real refiners blend ores, scrap and contracts. Read it as the scale of the refiner illusion, not a customs-grade origin. Computed by build_origin.py.

How to read it, and what it cannot show

Gross tonnes, not content. Tonnages are gross weights as traded, not contained metal, so they cannot be set against mine output or turned into a share of China’s supply. Rows differ in what they count: rare earths are compounds and metals (HS 2805.30 also includes scandium and yttrium), not ore; hafnium is measured as zircon tonnage, which says nothing about hafnium content; fluorspar adds both acid and metallurgical grades.

Partner, not mine. The partner is the country China’s customs declarations record, which may be a miner, a processor or a transit country — for example Malaysia (rare-earth processing), Thailand (antimony ores) or Lebanon (phosphate rock) may not be where the material was mined. Several partners are sanctions-relevant: North Korea and Russia supply tungsten ores, and Myanmar appears in rare earths, antimony, tungsten and baryte; trade with North Korea rests on China’s declarations alone.

Cross-check. CEPII BACI, which reconciles exporter and importer declarations, is within 2.5% of China’s own total for 8 of 13 rows in 2024. It differs, BACI relative to China’s total, for hafnium (-40%); titanium (-31%); antimony (-4%); feldspar (+3%); graphite (+16%). For hafnium BACI also leads with South Africa (41%), where China’s record leads with Australia. For titanium BACI puts Mozambique at 21%, against 45% in China’s record. Partner shares also differ in places; BACI’s leading partners for every row are in the JSON. The table uses China’s own record because the question is where China’s imported feed comes from. Comtrade flags estimated weights on some of China’s 2024 rows by partner and customs code; the most are on rare earths (40 rows) and graphite (10 rows).

Two endpoints. 2017 and 2024 are endpoints of a series that is not smooth; the full 2017–2024 series is in the JSON. These records fall below half of both neighbouring years; treat them as suspect: the 2023 record for aluminium (28.8 Mt; neighbours 125.5 Mt in 2022 and 158.6 Mt in 2024; weights estimated by Comtrade); the 2022 record for phosphate (32 kt; neighbours 68 kt in 2021 and 1,430 kt in 2023); the 2022 record for fluorspar (279 kt; neighbours 668 kt in 2021 and 1,017 kt in 2023); the 2021 record for baryte (35 kt; neighbours 87 kt in 2020 and 80 kt in 2022); the 2020 record for graphite (26 kt; neighbours 197 kt in 2019 and 60 kt in 2021).
