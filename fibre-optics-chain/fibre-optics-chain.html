Optical fibre · Research note

Germanium in optical fibre and the suppliers of submarine cables

Standard optical fibre has a germanium-doped core. The quantity is small and known only from estimates. Fibre for the longest submarine cables is offered with a pure silica core. Four suppliers built 83% of submarine cable length between 2020 and 2024.

Evidence review · 25 November 2026 · Claim register

Optical fibre is a strand of very pure silica glass that carries data as light. In standard fibre the core is doped with germanium, a metal that China has placed under export licensing. This note sets out how much germanium fibre uses, what is known about germanium supply, which fibre is made without germanium in the core, and who supplies submarine cable systems.

Finding. A US Geological Survey model puts the germanium in the core of single-mode fibre at 0.0039 grams per kilometre and in multimode fibre at 0.19 grams, about 49 times as much. On that model the fibre made in the United States in 2021 contained about 5 tonnes of germanium, nearly all of it in multimode fibre. World germanium supply is known only from estimates, and those put China's share of refining between about three-quarters and 94%. Fibre for the longest submarine cables is offered with a core of pure silica, which contains no germanium. A European Commission expert group reports that only three firms currently make fibre for submarine systems with repeaters, and that four suppliers built 83% of submarine cable length between 2020 and 2024. USGS Open-File Report 2024-1057. European Commission expert group, Annex, October 2025.

How much germanium is in fibre

Germanium dioxide raises the refractive index of the glass in the core, which keeps the light inside it. The only public estimate of the quantity found for this review is a USGS model of fibre made in the United States. It counts the core only and assumes that the core glass is 4% germanium dioxide, or 2.8% germanium. USGS Open-File Report 2024-1057.

The total is 4.97 tonnes: 0.07 tonnes in single-mode fibre and 4.9 tonnes in multimode fibre. Multimode fibre has a much wider core. The International Telecommunication Union's manual describes it as used in cabling inside premises, over lengths of 300 to 2,000 metres, and says single-mode fibre has almost completely replaced it in public networks. The estimate therefore rests on the assumed share of multimode fibre more than on anything else. ITU, Optical fibres, cables and systems.

More germanium is bought than ends up in the glass. USGS, citing a 2015 study, reports that 8% to 15% of the germanium fed to the process is incorporated in the fibre, that 80% to 95% of the rest is recovered from the waste, and that 60% of the germanium used comes from recycled material. It puts the primary germanium needed at 2.68 tonnes per tonne in the fibre, which gives 13.3 tonnes for the United States in 2021. USGS Open-File Report 2024-1057.

Fibre is a large share of germanium use. USGS last reported a split for the United States in 2016, when fibre took 40%, and lists fibre first among US uses in its 2026 edition. For the world it quotes an industry data provider's estimate of 30% in 2020 to 2022. USGS Open-File Report 2024-1057. USGS, germanium, 2026 edition.

The result ranges thirty-fold with the multimode share. A second estimate gives a point of comparison. If fibre takes 30% of world germanium, and world primary output is the 210 tonnes that USGS estimates for 2022, fibre uses about 63 tonnes a year. If that is primary germanium, the per-kilometre figures would need multimode fibre to be about 23% of world fibre length. No source opened for this review gives the multimode share of world output, so the two estimates cannot be reconciled here. Either multimode fibre is a larger share than long-distance use suggests, or fibre holds more germanium than a core-only count, or one of the estimates is too high.

World germanium supply is estimated, not measured

The USGS annual summary for germanium gives no world production figure and no share for China. Its 2026 edition says that data were limited and that available estimates were difficult to verify. Two other USGS reports give estimates. The first puts world primary production in 2022 at 210 tonnes: 180 in China, 20 in Canada, 6 in Russia and 4 in Belgium, which makes China's share 86% and leaves 30 tonnes outside China. It excludes recycled metal and describes the figures as highly uncertain. The second gives Chinese refined output of 154 tonnes in 2023 and a share of 77%, and states that the share rests on an assumed world total of 200 tonnes carried forward from 2020. It gives Chinese capacity as 262 tonnes. The International Energy Agency's 2026 outlook gives China 94% of refining in 2025 in one table, and plots a share of about three-quarters in a chart elsewhere in the same report. The two exhibits appear to be on different bases. The previous version of this page gave about 60% and attributed it to USGS, which publishes no such figure. USGS, germanium, 2026 edition. USGS Open-File Report 2024-1057. USGS Open-File Report 2026-1018. IEA, Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026.

China introduced export licensing for germanium in August 2023 and in December 2024 ruled out exports to the United States. USGS, germanium, 2026 edition. The part of the December 2024 measure that ruled out approval for US-bound shipments has since been suspended, at present through 27 January 2027, and the licensing requirement remains. The export-controls tracker gives the notices and follows the status.

China's reported exports of germanium metal in the first nine months fell from 36,656 kilograms in 2023 to 7,520 kilograms in 2025, a fall of 79%. The 2025 exports went mainly to Russia (28%), Belgium (26%), Germany (26%) and Japan (18%). The average price rose from USD 1,392 per kilogram in 2023 to an estimated USD 4,100 in 2025. US imports of germanium metal fell by 67% in 2025. The International Energy Agency reports that European germanium prices are around three times Chinese domestic prices, and that a refinery planned by Korea Zinc, with capacity for 54 tonnes of germanium a year, could double what the agency calls diversified supply. That is consistent with the USGS estimate of 30 tonnes produced outside China. USGS, germanium, 2026 edition. IEA, Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026.

USGS reports that fibre makers consume germanium as dioxide and tetrachloride, and that a company in Quapaw, Oklahoma, makes germanium tetrachloride for fibre from imported and recycled material. The quantities of tetrachloride made for fibre that were reported to the US Environmental Protection Agency were 9.8 to 13.7 tonnes of germanium a year in 2016 to 2019. USGS presumes these to be recycled manufacturing scrap and considers the reported totals a likely undercount. In 2021 to 2024 US imports of germanium dioxide came mostly from Belgium (57%) and Canada (37%), and imports of metal from China (41%), Belgium (27%) and Germany (25%). USGS Open-File Report 2024-1057. USGS, germanium, 2026 edition.

Fibre without germanium in the core

A germanium-doped core is not the only design. The International Telecommunication Union's manual describes the simplest fibre as a germanium-doped core inside pure silica cladding, and shows an alternative design with an undoped core. Sumitomo Electric describes its fibre of that kind as a core of pure silica inside cladding doped with fluorine, which lowers the refractive index of the cladding. ITU, Optical fibres, cables and systems. Sumitomo Electric, SEI Technical Review No. 86.

The alternative is established in submarine cables, where the lowest signal loss matters most. Sumitomo Electric states that it brought pure-silica-core fibre to market in 1988 and that such fibre has contributed to the development of submarine cable networks. Its current data sheets give the core glass as pure silica for its low-loss submarine fibres, including the one for ocean-crossing systems of 6,000 to 12,000 kilometres, and as germanium-doped silica for its lower-cost fibre for regional systems. Lightera, the name under which OFS now trades, describes its ocean fibre as having a pure silica core. Corning describes its submarine fibre as “silica core”, and a 2020 journal article describes one of its products as a pure-silica-core fibre. Sumitomo Electric, SEI Technical Review No. 86. Sumitomo Electric, Z Fiber data sheets. Lightera, TeraWave SCUBA 125. Corning, Vascade EX3000 product sheet. Morana and others (2020).

The International Energy Agency lists “germanium for high-performance fibre optics” among uses with limited options for substitutes. The manufacturers' documents show that at the highest performance the core is made without it. What they do not show is quantity: no source opened for this review gives the share of fibre, submarine or other, that has a pure silica core. The telecommunication standard that covers fibre for amplified submarine and long-distance land systems, G.654, sets geometrical, optical and mechanical characteristics and does not specify the glass. It says only that the substances should be indicated. IEA, Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026. ITU-T Recommendation G.654.

Who makes fibre

No free, authoritative source gives world fibre output by country. A brokerage estimate quoted in the press puts China at 59% of preform output, 57% of fibre output and 47% of cable output in 2025. The preform is the glass rod from which fibre is drawn. These are reported estimates and are not used in the calculations above. South China Morning Post, 8 May 2026.

For submarine fibre the supplier base is narrower. The European Commission's expert group reports that only three companies currently make fibre for submarine systems with repeaters, which it defines as long-distance systems: Corning and OFS in the United States and Sumitomo Electric in Japan. It adds that all three can make ultra-low-loss fibre, which no European manufacturer can currently match. European Commission expert group, Annex, October 2025.

Four suppliers built 83% of the cable length: ASN (33.7%), SubCom (23.7%), NEC (15.1%) and HMN Tech (10.2%). Short systems without repeaters, built by smaller suppliers, account for 17.1%, and Xtera for 0.2%. The three large suppliers outside China built 73%. The concentration index (HHI, zero to 10,000) of these shares is at least 2,031; the smaller suppliers are not listed one by one. The report gives ASN's ownership as 80% French state and 20% Nokia. European Commission expert group, Annex, October 2025.

Supplying a system and laying it are different activities. SubCom and ASN own cable-laying vessels. NEC and HMN Tech own none and contract the work out. The report counts 53 vessels worldwide that can install or maintain long submarine cables, 24 for laying and 29 for repair. SubCom and ASN own half of the cable-laying vessels. The rest belong mostly to telecommunication carriers and independent marine contractors. The report adds that vessels have to be reserved years in advance and that much of SubCom's fleet is in practice reserved for Google's cables. European Commission expert group, Annex, October 2025.

A 2026 study of ownership and supply, based on an open dataset, finds that US and Chinese state interventions are particularly pronounced in the supply sector, which it describes as an oligopoly where corporate and state interests align more strongly. Abels and Babić (2026).

What the customs records cover

The trade table uses three customs codes. One covers optical fibres, bundles and cables that are not made up of individually sheathed fibres. One covers cables made up of individually sheathed fibres, and does not separate land cable from submarine cable. The third is a group of minor metals in unwrought form, waste and powder: germanium is in it with gallium, indium, vanadium, niobium and others, and cannot be separated. Germanium dioxide is classified with zirconium dioxide, and germanium tetrachloride with other chlorides. No six-digit code isolates germanium in any form, and the extract has no code for preforms. WCO, HS 2017, Chapter 90. WCO, HS 2017, Chapter 85. WCO, HS 2017, Chapter 81. WCO, HS 2017, Chapter 28.

What remains open

Four quantities would settle the questions this note leaves open, and none was found as a measured figure in a public source. The first is world germanium production, for which only estimates exist. The second is the share of world fibre that is multimode, which decides how much germanium fibre uses. The third is the share of fibre made with a pure silica core. The fourth is world fibre and preform output by country from a statistical source. The supplier shares for submarine systems are estimates by length for one five-year period, and say nothing about systems now on order.

Revision note · 25 November 2026

The previous overview was headed “The internet's backbone is a trace of germanium, drawn by a handful of firms”. It gave China about 60% of refined germanium and labelled the figure measured. It said four firms build essentially all submarine cables, that three of them had more than 60% of 2024 revenue, and that about 99% of intercontinental data crosses on submarine fibre. It named a different set of fibre makers. This revision replaces the germanium share with the published range and its basis, adds the USGS quantity model, records that fibre for the longest submarine cables is offered without germanium in the core, and replaces the supplier claims with the European Commission expert group's figures. The revenue and data-traffic figures were not checked and are not carried forward. The claim register lists each change.
