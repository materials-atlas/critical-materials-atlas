Nuclear fuel supply

What makes nuclear fuel supply hard to replace?

Enrichment capacity is concentrated among a few operators. What utilities can replace after a supply interruption also depends on contracts, usable stocks and fuel qualification.

Evidence release · 24 November 2026

Fuel-cycle stages EU and US exposure Supply disruptions Methods Sources

Executive summary

Global enrichment capacity is more concentrated by operator than conversion capacity in the public tables used here. Russia’s share of enrichment capacity worldwide is considerably larger than its share of enrichment services purchased by US utilities or delivered to EU utilities. The two disruption scenarios calculate how much qualified replacement supply would have to arrive, and how much stock would have to cover the wait. Aggregate public data leave actual shortages and reactor impacts unresolved.

EU and US exposure follows procurement

European Union

Canada supplied 36.68% of the natural uranium delivered to EU utilities in 2025, Kazakhstan 20.31% and Russia 15.98%. Russian conversion services accounted for 24.40% of conversion deliveries. EU enrichers supplied 72.91% of enrichment services. ESA reports 42,522 tU of natural uranium equivalent in utility inventories at year end, including several forms of material and fresh fuel. Stage-specific stocks available to each affected utility depend on form, ownership and the reload schedule. ESA, 2025 Annual Report, Tables 3–5 and section 1.5.

By the end of 2025 all EU operators of Russian-designed VVER reactors had contracted an alternative fuel supplier. ESA still identified dependence on one fuel design and supplier at some utilities. Finland offers an observed substitution case. ESA reports that alternative fuel was loaded at Loviisa in 2025. The realised switch at that plant and the continuing qualification work elsewhere show why fleet-wide fabrication capacity gives an incomplete picture. ESA, 2025 Annual Report, section 2.1.3 and Finland country profile.

United States

Canada and Kazakhstan accounted for about 32% and 28% of all uranium purchased by US utilities for 2025 delivery, measured in pounds of uranium oxide equivalent. EIA withholds the 2025 Russia-origin amount in that table. EIA’s feed series identifies the destination enrichers and the uranium origin. A comparable volume by converter remains unavailable in this evidence. US-origin enrichment services made up 23.05% of purchases, with Russia at 25.84%. The EIA’s preliminary year-end inventory held by reactor owners and operators was 118.256 million pounds of uranium oxide equivalent across material types, out of 169.986 million pounds of total commercial inventories. The mass figure cannot establish what enrichment services utilities can replace. EIA Table 3, Table 13, Table 16 and Table 22.

US law restricts imports of covered Russian low-enriched uranium, with time-limited waivers ending no later than 1 January 2028. The 2025 purchase figures describe an observed delivery year within that transition. They give a historical basis for the scenario. Public Law 118–62, section 2.

What would it take to replace disrupted supply?

Figures in this section: modelled The baselines are observed 2025 and 2024 procurement; the outages, waiting times and stocks are assumptions.

Each scenario holds other deliveries fixed and uses observed 2025 procurements as an annual service baseline. A repeat using 2024 gives a historical sensitivity check. The model spreads supplies across each year and counts replacements only when they are eligible, incremental to existing commitments and able to arrive on time. Stock means a compatible opening reserve measured against the lost flow. The tables show assumptions for times and quantities with no probability attached. The modeled interruptions end at their stated horizons. If the disruption lasts longer and the stock has been used, maintaining service requires a continuing replacement rate equal to the lost flow.

Russian enrichment unavailable to the EU and US

The scenario removes Russian-origin enrichment services for 12 months. With no available stock, the two markets would jointly need 6.019 million SWU/year from immediate new deliveries on the 2025 baseline, compared with 5.493 million on the 2024 baseline. The joint hurdle counts each replacement delivery once. Shared suppliers such as Urenco and Orano would have to allocate incremental services across buyers. Their global rated capacity includes existing commitments. The analysis assigns no spare rate from any supplier, including suppliers focused on domestic markets.

A Western conversion facility is offline

The scenario assumes the EU loses the equivalent of its reported ConverDyn deliveries for 18 months, with the US facility serving those deliveries unavailable throughout. The gross EU loss is 4,237.5 tU on the 2025 baseline and 4,347.0 tU on 2024. The scenario covers the EU delivery lane. Other customers draw on the same pool of alternative converters. Any replacement from eligible suppliers requires available conversion slots and compatible input. A usable stock draw could bridge some demand if affected buyers can access material at the right stage. The model does not assign observed levels to either option. ESA Table 4 and ConverDyn’s facility relationship.

When does a simple ranking work?

Under zero replacement and equally absent stocks, the EU loses a larger fraction of its baseline enrichment services in the first example than of its conversion services in the second. In that comparison, the ranking agrees with the HHI ordering. The order reverses if enrichment losses can all be replaced immediately and conversion has no replacements and three months of usable opening stock. The EU would then have no enrichment service gap and a conversion service gap of 17.77% of baseline conversion deliveries over the scenario period. This is a conditional counterexample to the ranking. Public capacity and procurement tables are insufficient to determine which case maps to real contracts.

Methods and uncertainty

  • HHI sums the squares of percentage shares. Each row is an operator or the source’s small grouped residual. The top-three share uses the largest rows. Mining is reported as output by country and fabrication as listed capacity by plant location.
  • If an exposed annual flow is L and the scenario lasts T months, gross loss is L × T ÷ 12. If qualified new deliveries take d months to start, opening stock must first cover the loss through the earlier of arrival and scenario end. After that, the minimum annual rate is the remaining loss divided by the time left in the scenario expressed in years. If stock already covers the horizon, the rate is zero.
  • The calculation assumes steady service flows, fully fungible eligible replacements within each market and stock that is available when needed. It cannot resolve timing of individual refuelling cycles, price responses, shared transport, competing load schedules or qualification status by buyer. The scenarios stop at the supply of conversion or enrichment services. Reactor shutdown, generation and risk probability require further evidence.

The Atlas supplier-removal comparison uses the IEA’s 2025 screen. It removes Russia and China from both capacity and demand and puts remaining enrichment capacity at around 90% of remaining requirements (91% on its chart). The IEA estimates about 80% coverage for conversion on its corresponding basis. Here demand stays in the affected buyer markets and replacement is incremental to their remaining supply. IEA, p. 226.
