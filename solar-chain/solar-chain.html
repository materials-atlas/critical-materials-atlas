What limits solar module manufacturing?

Factories that assemble modules need compatible upstream inputs. Europe’s capacity estimates and a wafer supply stress test show where those limits can arise.

Evidence review · 24 November 2026 · Claim register

Calculated ceiling. Europe had about 33 GW per year of solar module manufacturing capacity in the IEA’s 2025 estimates. It had much less wafer capacity. With compatible equipment, full availability and a route with no imports or stocks at those manufacturing stages, the reported stages could support at most 1.5 GW per year of module-equivalent output, or about 4.5% of module nameplate capacity. The IEA figures omit ingots, so enforcing that additional step could lower the ceiling.

Capacity measures what factories are rated to make. Production measures what they actually make. The effect of a disruption also depends on qualified suppliers, inventories, utilization and how quickly alternative inputs can reach factories.

IEA, 29 July 2026. The commentary gives rounded figures for Europe. Its public materials do not specify country membership, conversion factors for the upstream rates, the share of operating lines, or technical compatibility.

A separate EU-27 inventory. In its August 2025 inventory, Fraunhofer lists no active ingot or wafer production in the EU-27 and identifies 0.4 GWp per year of ingot capacity closed in 2024–25. Its operating estimates for cells and modules were 2.9 and 6.7 GWp per year. Under that inventory, a route using only active EU-27 lines would need another source of ingots and wafers before it could operate. The narrower inventory cannot supply an ingot rate for the IEA’s wider Europe series. Fraunhofer ISE, p. 14.

Inverters. The IEA puts Europe’s inverter manufacturing capacity at about 95 GW per year in 2025 across solar PV and battery applications. An inverter converts electricity after installation. It sits outside the serial module manufacturing calculation. IEA, 29 July 2026.

A wafer supply stress test

Figures in this section: modelled The import rate, exposure, yields and stocks are assumptions, not observed data.

Illustrative scenarios. The model follows one compatible crystalline-silicon route through polysilicon, ingots, wafers, cells and modules. The six-month open case assumes wafer deliveries sufficient to fill cell capacity at the stated point assumptions. The shocks remove half or all of the assumed China-origin wafer lane. The model varies that lane’s exposure from zero to all imports and usable ingot capacity from zero to the rate needed to support the published wafer capacity. No observed sourcing or inventory data set these values.

What the EU-27 inventory can bound

Modelled from an operating-capacity inventory. In its August 2025 EU-27 inventory, Fraunhofer lists no active ingot or wafer plants. With no stocks or other imported intermediates, the compatible route yields 0 GW over 6 months without wafer deliveries. Supplying qualified wafers at an annual rate of 2.9 GW-equivalent would support up to 1.45 GW in the same period, assuming full availability, compatibility and unit yields. This example uses the EU-27 inventory alone. The delivery rate is hypothetical. Fraunhofer ISE, p. 14.

What a supply agreement can establish

In 2022, Meyer Burger announced a binding agreement for wafers from Norwegian Crystals and reported initial deliveries for its heterojunction production route. Norway sits outside the EU-27. The company gave no delivery volume, and IEA PVPS records that Norwegian Crystals ceased operations in 2023. In Spain, Sunwafe described an integrated ingot and wafer project with commercial operations targeted for 2029. These examples distinguish a historical supply lane from proposed capacity. Estimating replacement supply today requires evidence of operating lines, qualified buyers and available shipments. Meyer Burger release, IEA PVPS Norway report and Sunwafe announcement.

At the severe case’s point assumptions, adding capacity at either the ingot or wafer stage alone leaves the other stage limiting supply. Adding one GW per year at both raises output by 0.5 GW over the six-month shock. Imports of qualified cells could bypass the wafer and cell stages. This stress test holds that route outside the calculation so it isolates exposure to the wafer lane.

Comparing ways to preserve output

Figures in this section: modelled The same assumed model as the stress test above.

In the severe case, preserving 80% of the open point output means producing 4 GW over the shock. The model requires 3.25 GW-equivalent of additional compatible wafers. This puts manufacturing, qualified imports and inventories on the same physical target.

  • Integrated production. Add 6.5 GW-equivalent per year of wafer throughput and the ingot supply to support it before the shock.
  • Diversified sourcing. Arrange for suppliers outside the affected lane to deliver 3.25 GW-equivalent of qualified wafers during the shock.
  • Stocks. Hold 3.25 GW-equivalent of compatible wafers available at the start. The reserve is used once and must be replenished.

The IEA estimates that EU production with North African wafers could cost almost 20% less than fully EU-made modules under its same-size facility assumptions. A cost ranking at equal resilience would also need qualified capacity, delivery terms, inventory carrying costs and the length and frequency of interruptions. IEA, Energy Technology Perspectives 2026, executive summary.
