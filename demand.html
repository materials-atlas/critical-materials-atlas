Method · demand · the squeeze

The squeeze falls on gallium, germanium, vanadium

Headline figures on this page: modelled

Lithium demand could rise 5.9× by 2040, the largest multiple here. Demand growth alone does not make the squeeze. Crossed with supply that cannot respond, the structural squeeze falls on gallium, germanium and vanadium: high demand, and no way to add supply quickly. The other pages here measure supply. This one measures demand: which technologies (batteries, magnets, chips, solar, the grid) pull hardest on each material, and how fast that pull is growing. Demand growth is then crossed with supply elasticity. That separates the materials whose supply cannot respond from those that need more mines.

How demand and the squeeze are estimated

For each material the atlas records the principal end-use sectors, the clean-energy share of demand, and a demand-growth multiple to ~2040 (demand in 2040 ÷ today). These rest on the IEA Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2024 (Announced-Pledges scenario) and USGS end-use data. The squeeze index is normalised demand growth × companionality. It is high only when demand is rising fast and supply is a by-product that cannot scale.

Scenario-dependence. A forward demand multiple depends on the scenario, so a single number hides the uncertainty. For the six minerals where the IEA publishes a total-demand series, the atlas does not curate a point estimate. It computes g in all three scenarios from the IEA Critical Minerals Dataset (CC BY 4.0): STEPS (stated policies), APS (announced pledges, the central case) and NZE (net zero), and carries the whole band into the squeeze index. The other materials keep a curated literature estimate, labelled as such in the table. The IEA’s 37-mineral sheet covers clean-tech demand only, which is a different quantity from total demand and would overstate g. Inputs: IEA Critical Minerals Dataset + USGS × companionality.json → demand.json.

What is actually doing the pulling?

A multiple says how much more demand there will be, not where it comes from. The IEA breaks demand down by end-use technology, so the growth can be decomposed: the biggest use by 2040, and the technology that adds the most absolute demand between 2024 and 2040 (the growth driver).

What this opens

The demand arm adds to the atlas a view of where pressure is heading, next to the record of where supply sits. Joined to the supply-structure work, it separates the materials that need capital and time (lithium, graphite: mine more) from those that need other tools (gallium, germanium, rare earths: recovery yield, stockpiles, substitution, because more mines are not an option). Three extensions follow: demand by technology scenario (what a faster EV path does to each squeeze score), demand by country or bloc (whose industrial policy pulls which metal), and coupling demand growth to the price series to test whether the squeeze already shows in unit values.
