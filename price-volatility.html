Are by-product metals really more volatile?

Yes, but not for the usual reason. On 24 years of real prices, the volatility of by-product metals is fully accounted for by how small their markets are. Small primary metals (rare earths, tantalum, beryllium) swing just as hard, and companionality cannot explain those. A price is volatile because its market is small, not because its supply is tied to a host metal.

How this test is built (and what it fixes)

Prices. USGS Historical Statistics for Mineral and Material Commodities (Data Series 140): real annual unit values in constant 1998 dollars, public domain, one series per commodity. That matters: the atlas’s earlier price test used trade unit values, where gallium, germanium and hafnium share a single HS6 code (811292) and therefore carry identical prices. Here each is priced separately, so the problem does not arise.

Window: 2000–2023. The choice of window is itself a result. Before ~2000 the USGS nominal series for minor metals are administered list prices, frozen for years at a stretch (germanium 13 years, hafnium 11, helium 9, gallium 8). Deflating a frozen nominal price creates artificial “real” volatility from the CPI. That artefact would fall on the by-product metals and bias the coefficient under test. After 2000 every series is market-priced. Helium remains partly administered (US Federal Helium Reserve) and is flagged.

Model. Volatility = standard deviation of year-on-year log returns of the real price. Then OLS with heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors: volatility ~ by-product status, then the same plus log market size. Classification is the textbook primary/companion split (USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries; Nassar, Graedel & Alonso 2015). It is a binary class, not an invented fraction. Metals whose class is disputed are flagged and dropped in a robustness model.

HC3, not HC0. HC0 is biased downward in small samples: it under-states standard errors and over-states significance. The standard advice is HC3 whenever n < 250 (MacKinnon & White 1985; Long & Ervin 2000). At n=33, HC0 would overstate the significance of every number on this page, including the results it reports. Everything here uses HC3, which makes the results less significant, not more.

Limits: USGS unit values are US-market annual averages, not global spot. Excluded: mercury (class disputed), fluorspar and niobium (too few price years), hafnium and titanium (no world-production series, so no size control). Input: USGS DS-140 → price_volatility.json.

The whole finding, in one chart

Left = a smaller market. Up = a more volatile price. If companionality drove volatility, the red points would sit above the blue ones at the same market size. They do not. They sit on the same downward line, towards its small end. Market size explains the pattern; the by-product metals are concentrated at the small end of it.

Add market size and it falls by about 90% and loses all significance, while size itself is strongly significant and the model’s explanatory power more than doubles. The strongest objection to this page

“Market size is a bad control.” The objection runs like this: if being a by-product causes a market to be small (because output is capped by what the host mine produces), then size sits on the causal path rather than beside it. Controlling for it would then subtract companionality’s own mechanism from companionality’s effect, and the null result would mean “no direct effect”, not “companionality is irrelevant.” The objection is valid, and further regressions cannot answer it. Three responses follow, from weakest to strongest:

1. The total effect is reported. It is , and it is the headline of this page, as the objection requires. By-product metals are more volatile. This page explains that fact; it does not deny it.

2. Size measured before the fact. If volatility keeps markets small (rather than the reverse), the control is contaminated. So the model was re-run with market size measured in 1985–1999 and volatility in 2000–2023: volatility in this century cannot have caused market size in the last one.

3. Small primary metals. The objection does not apply here. Rare earths, tantalum and beryllium are primary metals, whose supply does not depend on a host, and they are among the most volatile prices in the dataset. Their markets are small for ordinary reasons: demand is low. Mediation cannot explain them, because there is no companionality in the chain to mediate. They show that a small market is sufficient for volatility on its own. Once smallness alone produces the swing, by-product status has nothing left to explain.

What this design cannot say. None of this rules out that companionality contributes to a market being small in the first place. That channel is real in principle (no more gallium can be made than alumina refining yields), and this design says nothing about it. The claim is therefore narrow: there is no volatility penalty specific to being a by-product, over and above being small. Whether companionality helps make these markets small is a separate question, and a worthwhile one.

“But n=33 is small”

It is, and it cannot be enlarged, because n=33 is not a small sample of a large universe. It is most of the universe. Only about forty metals have a published price series at all, and this test uses thirty-three of them. No larger dataset can be found, because no further metals have a price series. The same bound applies to the published literature.

The response is to avoid relying on a large n. Every result here is re-tested three more ways that do not lean on large-sample asymptotics: a permutation test (shuffle the by-product labels 20,000 times; no normality assumed), a rank-based model (unaffected by the large leverage of bulk commodities: iron ore is 109 tonnes, rhenium 101.7), and drop-one, which removes the metals that could carry the result on their own. They agree.

Does the answer depend on the window?

A fair worry: 24-year volatility might hide episodic spikes. With non-overlapping windows the direction holds at every length (bigger markets are calmer, and by-product status is never close to significant),

volatility windowobservationsby-productpmarket sizep
Why a panel does not help. The obvious way to get a bigger n is a panel: slice the window into rolling 5-year sub-periods and there are 614 observations instead of 33. In that panel by-product status becomes significant (+7.8pp, p=0.07) while market size goes away. This is an artefact, for two reasons. First, rolling windows overlap, so each year’s return is reused up to four times: the 2023 gallium/germanium export-control spike is counted repeatedly, and pseudo-replication looks like evidence. Run the same 5-year window without overlap and the effect vanishes (+1.5pp, p=0.70) while size returns (p=0.003). Second, by-product status never varies within a metal, so a panel adds no information about it: the naive unclustered panel reports p<0.0001, but with the errors clustered by metal, as they must be, the effective sample is back to 33. The panel does not add evidence. It counts the same evidence more times.

How this sits with the literature

The direct precedent is Redlinger & Eggert, “Volatility of by-product metal and mineral prices” (Resources Policy 47, 2016, 69–77), which reports by-products averaging ~50% higher volatility across ~50 years of annual prices. The uncontrolled estimate here reproduces that direction and is significant. The controlled estimate does not.

That comparison needs care. Their variables tested included quantity produced, so this is not a claim that they missed the control. Their full specification is paywalled and has not been read for this page, and the window here (24 years) and the sample differ from theirs. They raised both of this page’s findings themselves: they attribute the “mixed evidence” in their monthly data to “the smaller volume of transactions for by-product materials” and to prices “unchanged for several months at a time”, that is, thinness and administered prices. And they close by calling for research into “the underlying determinants of price volatility.” This page responds to that call, on fully open data: among the candidate determinants, market size absorbs the by-product effect entirely. Their own suspected explanation appears to be the right one.

Why this matters for the rest of the atlas

This result does not weaken the hostage-metals thesis; it narrows it. The claim that by-product supply cannot respond to price is a statement about elasticity, and it stands on its own evidence: a higher gallium price does not lead to a gallium mine. What fails is the corollary that inelasticity should therefore show up as price volatility. It does not, once market size is accounted for. Inelasticity and volatility are different claims, and treating them as one let a size effect pass for a structural one. The risk re-weighting, which penalises companionality via elasticity rather than via observed volatility, is unaffected, and this result supports keeping it that way.
