Method · production · cross-source validation

Production in tonnes

Every other page works in shares or trade value. This one adds absolute physical production from two more independently compiled sources. World Mining Data (Austrian ministry) gives mine output in metric tonnes; the British Geological Survey’s World Mineral Statistics adds a third count. Set beside the atlas’s USGS-derived shares, they show the scale of each market and test whether the producer geography holds up when two other compilations count it separately. Where the sources disagree, the two independent compilations decide the case.

The three sources, and how the check works

Absolute production: World Mining Data 6.4 (2026 ed.), production by country in metric tonnes, 2024. For each material the page takes the world total and the top producer’s tonnage share, and compares that share with the atlas’s existing USGS-derived top-producer share (in data.json). A third source, the British Geological Survey’s World Mineral Statistics (via its free OGC API, latest year per commodity), gives an independent third count for 18 of the materials. The same top country across sources, with a small share gap, means that independently compiled sources agree.

How independent the sources are. The usual objection to any second-source check is that the second source repackages the first. World Mining Data tags every figure with its origin, so the objection can be tested. Across 1,903 tagged figures in the 2024 edition: national statistics 60.0%, company reports 28.5%, questionnaire 6.7%, IEA 1.5%, ICG 1.1%, USGS 0.8% (16 figures), BP 0.7%, Kimberley 0.6%, WNA 0.2%. WMD is therefore not a repackaging of USGS, and the circularity objection fails in its usual form.

What the agreement can show. A weaker version of the objection is right, and it limits what this page can claim. Both compilations rest on the same upstream: national statistical returns and company reports. They are independent compilations, not independent measurements. If a country misreports its output, both inherit the error and agree perfectly; agreement would then be evidence of nothing. So what 26/28 demonstrates is compilation reliability: two teams, working separately from the same primary returns, made the same call. That has value, but it is not measurement validation, and this page no longer claims it is. No open source independently measures mine output. The closest the atlas has is the satellite footprint layer, which sees area, not tonnes. Area is measured from orbit and owes nothing to any national statistic, so it is the one fully independent check available, though a coarse one.

The satellite check. It tests whether a country’s share of the world’s mapped mine footprint tracks its share of bulk open-pit output. This can only corroborate the aggregate mining geography: the polygons are undifferentiated coal/metal/aggregate, and area-per-tonne varies by orders of magnitude, so it cannot validate any per-metal share. For the coarse claim “these are the world’s big mining nations”, it is the one check that does not rest on the same national returns as USGS and WMD, and it agrees.

How far the other agency sits from these figures. This page already shows the three compilations side by side; this is how far apart two of them sit, measured. For the commodities where the atlas has measured it, the USGS world total for the same commodity and stage differs from the BGS one by a median of: graphite 30.7%, antimony 13.1%, cobalt 12.7%, tungsten 6.9%, rare earths 5.7%, copper 0.6% (median absolute difference over 2002–2024, unsigned). That is a disagreement between two compilations, not an error bar and not a correction of either — it can be measurement, definition, coverage or vintage. The same measurement also shows how far the USGS moves its own current-year estimate, which is the figure to check before treating a how firm is a current-year production figure?

Caveats: the sources define commodities slightly differently (e.g. contained-metal vs concentrate, ore vs oxide), report different years’ vintages, and treat re-processing differently, so a few points of share difference are expected. Real disagreements are flagged. On bauxite and baryte the two independents (World Mining Data and BGS) agree with each other and put a different country on top than the atlas’s USGS figure (Guinea for bauxite, China for baryte); there the third source decides the case. Titanium splits three ways on the ilmenite-vs-slag definition. Coverage: 28 materials have a WMD sheet; BGS holds a panel for 62 commodities; of the atlas’s materials, 25 have a recent (≥2020) BGS mine series in the cube. Silicon and helium have no BGS panel. Gallium and germanium do have BGS series (Production of primary gallium and Germanium metal), but they are refinery output, not mining, so they are absent from the mine-stage comparison above rather than from BGS; niobium shares a panel with tantalum. (Two earlier versions of this note were wrong in opposite directions: one said gallium, germanium and the PGMs had no BGS series, the other said niobium and tantalum had none.) What this changes for the atlas

With absolute tonnages the derived layers can speak in quantities: a cascade shock becomes “so many tonnes of world gallium”, not an index; the demand arm can be normalised to physical supply. Two more independently compiled authorities (World Mining Data and the British Geological Survey) put the same country on top for of the materials all three can check, so the producer geography the atlas has asserted all along is not a USGS artefact. Where they disagree, the disagreement is shown and named. On bauxite and baryte the two independents agree with each other against USGS, which is the kind of signal a triangulation is meant to surface.
