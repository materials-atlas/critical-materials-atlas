Heat pumps · Components and assembly

What limits heat-pump supply?

Europe has substantial heat-pump assembly capacity. Compatible compressors can still limit factory output, while trained installers affect how quickly equipment reaches buildings.

Evidence review · 25 November 2026 · Claim register

The European Commission reports spare heat-pump assembly capacity, especially for hydronic units, alongside gaps in compressor and four-way valve production. The IEA finds that rotary compressor capacity is highly concentrated globally, while scroll compressor capacity is more geographically diverse. The question is how much factory output a disruption would put at risk for a line that needs a specific compressor. European Commission, 2026 Industrial Accelerator Act impact assessment, Figure HP. IEA, Heat Pump Monitor 2026, p. 32.

The JRC inventory identifies copper and, in some designs, neodymium in compressor motors. It describes generally limited material-related vulnerabilities and identifies risks from component disruptions and metal prices. Establishing a particular raw-material constraint would require material demand and available supply by grade and application. JRC, Heat Pumps in the European Union, 2025, Tables 5–6 and §4.4.

The Kigali Amendment phases down HFCs globally. EU Regulation 2024/573 sets separate rules for placing equipment on the EU market. For example, Annex IV limits fluorinated gases with a global warming potential of 150 or higher in split air-to-water units up to 12 kW from January 2027, subject to safety exceptions and specific derogations. Different refrigerants can require different compressors, controls and safety provisions. Trained technicians are a deployment constraint documented separately by the IEA. Capacity depends on the product and the year

The IEA estimates global manufacturing capacity for building heat pumps at about 145 GW per year in 2024. More than 70% of sales were supplied locally in each major market. Local sourcing refers to the finished equipment and gives no breakdown of component origins. IEA, Heat Pump Monitor 2026, pp. 23–24.

Calculated comparison. The JRC’s 2025 mixed-technology EU estimate was 7.7 million units per year. At its assumed 10 kW per unit, this is 77 GW of thermal nameplate output per year. Holding that estimate fixed, it covers about 117% of the rounded 66 GW annual requirement in the JRC’s flat path and 77% of the 100 GW requirement in 2030 on its growth path. The two deployment paths are modelled. Meeting an annual average leaves open whether capacity is sufficient in the peak year. These JRC quantities use their own technology coverage and conversion. Qualified compressors can limit assembly

The IEA locates about 90% of global rotary compressor manufacturing capacity in China in 2024. The category includes applications such as air conditioning. Its chart shows more geographically dispersed scroll capacity. Neither category isolates compatible volumes for EU residential air-to-water lines. IEA, Heat Pump Monitor 2026, p. 32.

Copeland reports that a Weishaupt residential air-to-water design uses its variable-speed R290 scroll compressor with a qualified drive and controller. A separate Copeland–Daikin announcement describes a swing rotary and controls offering for European residential heat pumps. These disclosures identify different component configurations and give no qualified delivery volumes for the disruption case. Copeland, Weishaupt residential air-to-water case. Copeland–Daikin European cooperation announcement, December 2025.

When a compressor interruption cuts factory output

Modelled. The illustration follows one EU-27 residential air-to-water line over 8 months. All numbers in the stress test are assumptions or calculations from them. Monthly orders are 1,000 units and assembly can make 1,200. Normal monthly arrivals are 400 domestic and 600 imported compressors. The design uses 1 compressor per unit. For 6 months the example removes 50% of the 300 imports on an assumed China-origin route, cutting regular arrivals by 150 per month. The line starts with 300 compressors in stock. Alternatives can supply 100 per month after 3 full months of qualification. All other inputs are assumed available.

With 300 compressors in stock, the grid shows how earlier qualification can cover a temporary gap. The downloadable results also vary qualified replacement from 0 to 150 compressors per month and orders from 1,000 to 1,200 units per month. Across that full set the loss against a matched no-shock case ranges from 0 to 900 units. High demand can cause unmet orders even in the no-shock case. The range describes the chosen scenarios and assigns no probabilities. Each month’s balance counts stock once and lets unchanged arrivals resume after the shock.

Calibrating the model would require the component mix by design, actual procurement by origin, opening stocks, qualified spare capacity and approval timelines. Production and unmet factory orders are the outputs. Installed heat pumps and heating delivered additionally depend on local installers, demand and building conditions.
