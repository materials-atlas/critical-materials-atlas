# Literature base: "Does the grid bind before the chip?"

Compiled 18 Sep 2026. **Every source below was opened and read on 2026-09-18** (the access date for all entries), either as the full PDF (downloaded with curl and converted to text, so quotes are verbatim) or as the publisher/repository landing page (WebFetch). The **Access** line says which. "PDF p." is the page in the PDF file; "printed p." is the number printed on the page, where they differ. Sources that could not be opened are listed in the last section and are not used above.

Tiers: **[O]** official (government agency, national laboratory, international agency) · **[P]** peer-reviewed journal · **[W]** working paper or preprint (not peer reviewed) · **[I]** industry or trade body, used only where a public text exists, and never as the sole support for a claim.

## Group 1: Transformer and GOES supply record, 2020-2025

### 1.1 [O] DOE (2024), Large Power Transformer Resilience, Report to Congress
- **Citation:** U.S. Department of Energy (2024). *Large Power Transformer Resilience: Report to Congress*. July 2024 (signed by Secretary Granholm 10 Jul 2024). https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2024-10/EXEC-2022-001242%20-%20Large%20Power%20Transformer%20Resilience%20Report%20signed%20by%20Secretary%20Granholm%20on%207-10-24.pdf
- **Access:** full PDF. Printed p. = PDF p. minus 6.
- **Quotes:**
  - "lead times for acquisition of an LPT have become exceptionally long, with 36-month lead times being commonly quoted and maximum lead times reaching as much as 60 months" (Executive Summary, printed p. 2).
  - "Prior to the Coronavirus Disease of 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, it was possible to place an order for a large transformer with a lead time of less than a year" (§III.3.1, printed p. 12).
  - "Cleveland-Cliffs (through its 2020 acquisition of AK Steel) is the sole active domestic GOES supplier. The company is able to meet between 12-20 percent of domestic GOES demand" (§III.3.3, printed p. 15; the dash is lost in the PDF text layer, which reads "1220").
  - "GOES and CTC each account for roughly 25 percent of final LPT production costs" (printed p. 12, citing the 2020 Commerce survey).
- **Use:** the official benchmark for the lead-time story: under a year before COVID, 36 months typical by 2024. It also documents the single US GOES producer.

### 1.2 [O] NIAC (2024), Addressing the Critical Shortage of Power Transformers
- **Citation:** President's National Infrastructure Advisory Council (2024). *Addressing the Critical Shortage of Power Transformers to Ensure Reliability of the U.S. Grid*. June 2024. Published by CISA. https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/2024-09/NIAC_Addressing%20the%20Critical%20Shortage%20of%20Power%20Transformers%20to%20Ensure%20Reliability%20of%20the%20U.S.%20Grid_Report_06112024_508c_pdf_0.pdf
- **Access:** full PDF.
- **Quotes:**
  - "an electric utility or generation developer that orders a transformer may have to wait 2 to 4 years for it to be delivered, compared to a wait of just months as recently as 2020" (Executive Summary, p. 3).
  - Quoting Wood Mackenzie (2023): "transformer lead times have been increasing for the last 2 years - from around 50 weeks in 2021, to 120 weeks on average in 2024. Large transformers, both substation power and generator step-up transformers, have lead times ranging from 80 to 210 weeks." (p. 3)
  - "Transformer prices are 80 percent higher since the beginning of the pandemic." (p. 3, based on the St. Louis Fed/BLS transformer price index, Figure 2, p. 4)
  - "The previous cyclical nature of the industry has made manufacturers wary of increasing capacity despite rising demand" (p. 5).
- **Use:** the most-cited compact statement of the shortage. It also makes the capacity point: manufacturers hesitated to add capacity, which is the mechanism the paper tests. The Wood Mackenzie figure is second-hand here (see "Could not verify").

### 1.3 [O] NREL (2024), Major Drivers of Long-Term Distribution Transformer Demand
- **Citation:** McKenna, K., Abraham, S. A., and Wang, W. (2024). *Major Drivers of Long-Term Distribution Transformer Demand*. NREL/TP-6A40-87653, National Renewable Energy Laboratory, February 2024. https://docs.nlr.gov/docs/fy24osti/87653.pdf (OSTI 2309697)
- **Access:** full PDF.
- **Quotes:**
  - "Distribution transformers ... are currently experiencing an unprecedented imbalance between supply and demand. Utilities are experiencing extended lead times for transformers of up to 2 years (a fourfold increase on pre-2022 lead times), and reporting price increases by as much as 4-9 times in the past 3 years" (p. 1).
  - Shortages are "attributed to pent-up post-pandemic demand; difficulty recruiting, training, and retaining a skilled workforce; component supply chain challenges; and materials shortages (grain-oriented electrical steel, aluminum, and copper)" (p. 1).
  - Capacity of the installed stock is expected to rise "up to a 160%-260% increase on 2021 levels" by 2050 (p. 7).
- **Use:** the distribution-transformer counterpart (HS 8504.21/22 are mostly distribution sizes). The "4-9 times" figure comes from utility reports of individual quotes, not a price index. Note that NREL's news page gives a different range ("5-6 times in 2 years"); the PDF above is the authoritative text.

### 1.4 [O] IEA (2025), Building the Future Transmission Grid
- **Citation:** International Energy Agency (2025). *Building the Future Transmission Grid: Strategies to Navigate Supply Chain Challenges*. IEA, Paris, 25 Feb 2025. Licence CC BY 4.0. https://www.iea.org/reports/building-the-future-transmission-grid ; PDF https://iea.blob.core.windows.net/assets/a688d0f5-a100-447f-91a1-50b7b0d8eaa1/BuildingtheFutureTransmissionGrid.pdf
- **Access:** full PDF and landing page.
- **Quotes:**
  - "it now takes two to three years to procure cables and up to four years to secure large power transformers. Average lead times for cables and large power transformers have almost doubled since 2021." (Executive summary, p. 8)
  - "prices for cables have nearly doubled since 2019, and the price of power transformers rose by around 75%" (p. 8; survey-based real price index, 2018 USD, chart p. 26).
  - "In some cases, transformer prices have surged to as much as 2.6 times their pre-pandemic levels, in real terms." (p. 26)
  - "GOES prices have doubled between 2021 and mid-2023 ... some manufacturers have shifted focus away from GOES production to meet NOES demand. China continues to be the main manufacturer, accounting for around a third of global production." (p. 25)
  - "Copper prices experienced an increase in 2021 and early 2022. However, from 2021 to 2023, supply grew at a faster pace than demand, leading to a decline and stabilisation of prices." (p. 25)
  - Average LPT lead time chart: "x 1.8" from 2021-22 to 2023-24 (p. 27). "Manufacturers have reported a record backlog of orders in 2024, with orders measured in USD increasing by one-third in just one year." Hitachi Energy backlog "more than triple in the past four years, now surpassing USD 30 billion" (pp. 27-28).
  - **Against a pure equipment story:** "while lead time increases have affected project timelines and costs, the primary cause of delays in transmission projects remains permitting, particularly in advanced economies." (p. 28)
- **Use:** the only global, survey-based price and lead-time series for power transformers, which fits the paper's world-trade scope. The copper sentence predicts a weak or absent signal for 7408.11 after 2022. The permitting sentence is a direct caveat to "the grid binds".

- **Quotes:**

### 1.6 [O] DOE (2022), Electric Grid Supply Chain Review: LPTs and HVDC
- **Citation:** U.S. Department of Energy (2022). *Electric Grid Supply Chain Review: Large Power Transformers and High Voltage Direct Current Systems. Supply Chain Deep Dive Assessment*. Response to EO 14017, 24 Feb 2022. https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2022-02/Electric%20Grid%20Supply%20Chain%20Report%20-%20Final.pdf (OSTI 1871501)
- **Access:** full PDF.
- **Quotes:**
  - "Stakeholders cited examples of lead time comparison before and after COVID-19 as follows: 5 weeks vs. 12 weeks for GOES, 8 weeks vs. 18 weeks for CTC, 4 weeks vs. 12 weeks for regular Cu sheet, and 6-week increase for tap changers." (§3.2.2, printed p. 51)
  - "stakeholders reported that on average, it takes 12 to 16 weeks to manufacture an LPT if all the materials are available." (PDF p. 36)
  - "It was estimated that in 2019, utilized domestic capacity was about 40%, and 82% of LPTs consumed in the United States were imported" (Executive Summary, PDF p. 8).
- **Use:** the gap between 12-16 weeks of manufacturing time and quoted lead times of 2 to 4 years shows the bottleneck is queueing and capacity, not production time. It also gives the only published input lead times for GOES and CTC. The 40% utilisation in 2019 is spare capacity going into the shock.

### 1.7 [O] U.S. Department of Commerce, BIS (2020), Section 232 report on transformers and components
- **Citation:** U.S. Department of Commerce, Bureau of Industry and Security, Office of Technology Evaluation (2020). *The Effect of Imports of Transformers and Transformer Components on the National Security*. - **Access:** full PDF.

### 1.11 [O] DOE (2024), Distribution Transformers Energy Conservation Standards, Final Rule

- **Access:** full pre-publication PDF. Printed p. = PDF p. minus 1.
- **Quotes:**
  - "the price of distribution transformers stayed relative constant for several years following the implementation of standards in 2016. It was not until late 2020 or early 2021, when significant disruptions to the market and industry-wide supply chain challenges began to occur, that distribution transformer prices began to significantly increase. These price increases were directly correlated to price increases for grain oriented electrical steel, which nearly doubled in price from 2021 to 2023." (printed pp. 226-227)

**Reading for the paper:** for large power transformers, GOES is about 20-25% of cost, copper conductor about 25%, and all materials together are over 50% (IEA 2025, NLR 2026). That is enough for a doubling of GOES prices (2021-23) to explain a large share of the transformer price rise through cost alone: 25% × 100% ≈ 25 percentage points of the ~63% PPI rise between 2019 and 2022. This is my back-of-envelope arithmetic, not a source claim. Oil and tank-steel cost shares for oil-filled units were **not found in any verified source**.

## Group 3: Method. Trade unit values as price measures, and their pitfalls

### 3.1 [P] Schott (2004)
- **Citation:** Schott, P. K. (2004). "Across-Product Versus Within-Product Specialization in International Trade." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 119(2): 647-678. doi:10.1162/0033553041382201
- **Access:** DOI metadata verified on Crossref. The publisher page did not render the abstract, so the quotes come from the working-paper version: NBER WP 8492 (Sept 2001), "Do Rich and Poor Countries Specialize in a Different Mix of Goods? Evidence from Product-Level US Trade Data", https://www.nber.org/papers/w8492
- **Quotes (NBER abstract):** "Unit values of US imports at the product level reveal a substantial degree of vertical product differentiation among countries exporting to the US"; "within-product unit value dispersion is positively and significantly correlated with source country income".
- **Use:** justifies measuring change **within** exporter × importer × product flows, not across them. A unit value's level reflects quality and the exporter's income, so only its change over time within a flow can stand in for a price.

### 3.2 [P] Hallak and Schott (2011)
- **Citation:** Hallak, J. C. and Schott, P. K. (2011). "Estimating Cross-Country Differences in Product Quality." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 126(1): 417-474. doi:10.1093/qje/qjq003
- **Access:** Crossref metadata, plus the abstract from NBER WP 13807 (Feb 2008), https://www.nber.org/papers/w13807
- **Quote:** the paper offers "a method for decomposing countries' observed export prices into quality versus quality-adjusted-price components using information contained in their trade balances."
- **Use:** a rise in unit value can be a rise in quality, not in price. This is directly relevant because efficiency standards (the DOE 2016 and 2029 rules) push transformer quality up.

### 3.3 [P] Khandelwal (2010)
- **Citation:** Khandelwal, A. (2010). "The Long and Short (of) Quality Ladders." *Review of Economic Studies* 77(4): 1450-1476. doi:10.1111/j.1467-937X.2010.00602.x
- **Access:** Crossref metadata, plus the abstract from NBER WP 15178 (July 2009), https://www.nber.org/papers/w15178
- **Quote:** "Prices are typically used as proxies for countries' export quality. I relax this strong assumption by exploiting both price and quantity information ... Higher quality is assigned to products with higher market shares conditional on price."
- **Use:** the joint price-quantity logic the paper uses can also be read as quality. The paper needs to say why the 2021-24 co-movement is not a quality upgrade, for example because it happens within flows and at the same time across all origins.

### 3.4 [P] Feenstra and Romalis (2014)
- **Citation:** Feenstra, R. C. and Romalis, J. (2014). "International Prices and Endogenous Quality." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 129(2): 477-527. doi:10.1093/qje/qju001
- **Access:** abstract from Crossref.
- **Quote:** "The unit values of internationally traded goods are heavily influenced by quality ... We find that quality-adjusted prices vary much less across countries than do unit values".
- **Use:** a second authority for the unit-value-is-not-price warning. It supports within-flow differencing and a control group of comparable goods.

### 3.5 [W] Silver (2007), IMF
- **Citation:** Silver, M. (2007). "Do Unit Value Export, Import, and Terms of Trade Indices Represent or Misrepresent Price Indices?" IMF Working Paper 07/121, May 2007. doi:10.5089/9781451866858.001 (published as *IMF Staff Papers* 56(2): 297-322, 2009 [P], doi:10.1057/imfsp.2008.24)
- **Access:** abstract from Crossref.
- **Quote:** "This paper provides evidence of substantial bias in their representation of such price changes. Their continued use would mislead economic analysis."
- **Use:** the standard official-statistics caution. The paper should cite it and answer it with within-flow, 6-digit, reconciled unit values plus a benchmark against the BLS transformer PPI (1.10).

### 3.6 [W] Gaulier and Zignago (2010), CEPII BACI documentation
- **Citation:** Gaulier, G. and Zignago, S. (2010). "BACI: International Trade Database at the Product-Level. The 1994-2007 Version." CEPII Working Paper 2010-23, October 2010. http://www.cepii.fr/PDF_PUB/wp/2010/wp2010-23.pdf (SSRN doi:10.2139/ssrn.1994500)
- **Access:** full PDF.
- **Quotes:**
  - BACI's advantages include "its unit values, which are more reliable than the raw data, since the reconciliation of mirror figures tend to correct discrepancies" (Abstract, p. 3).
  - "We regress the observed CIF/FOB ratios for a given flow on gravity variables and a product-specific world median unit value." (p. 4)
  - "Even though most quantities are reported in tons, there is 15% reported in other quantity units (units, meters, watt, etc) ... About 8,5% of final quantities in BACI have been converted using this method." (§2.2, pp. 11-12)
- **Use:** the documentation for the dataset. **Pitfall specific to this paper:** transformers (8504.2x) are often reported in number of units, which BACI converts to tonnes using mirror-flow conversion rates. Within-flow unit-value changes can therefore pick up conversion artefacts. The paper should check how sensitive results are to flows originally reported in tonnes. (This is the 2010 vintage; the current BACI methodology note, for the HS17/HS22 releases, should be cited alongside it.)

### 3.7 [W] Gaulier, Martin, Mejean and Zignago (2008/2012), CEPII TradePrices
- **Citation:** Gaulier, G., Martin, J., Méjean, I., and Zignago, S. "International Trade Price Indices." CEPII (SSRN doi:10.2139/ssrn.1994535).
- **Access:** abstract from Crossref.
- **Quote:** "We document here TradePrices, our database of aggregated and sectoral trade price indices for all countries, computed using unit values given by BACI".
- **Use:** precedent for building price indices from BACI unit values, including chained Fisher and Törnqvist forms.

## Group 4: Method. Separating supply from demand shocks using the joint movement of price and quantity

### 4.1 [P] Kilian (2009)
- **Citation:** Kilian, L. (2009). "Not All Oil Price Shocks Are Alike: Disentangling Demand and Supply Shocks in the Crude Oil Market." *American Economic Review* 99(3): 1053-1069. doi:10.1257/aer.99.3.1053
- **Access:** abstract from Crossref.
- **Quote:** "Shocks to the real price of oil may reflect oil supply shocks, shocks to the global demand for all industrial commodities, or demand shocks that are specific to the crude oil market. Each shock has different effects on the real price of oil".
- **Use:** the canonical case that a price rise alone does not identify its cause.

### 4.2 [P] Uhlig (2005)
- **Citation:** Uhlig, H. (2005). "What Are the Effects of Monetary Policy on Output? Results from an Agnostic Identification Procedure." *Journal of Monetary Economics* 52(2): 381-419. doi:10.1016/j.jmoneco.2004.05.007
- **Access:** metadata from Crossref and RePEc, plus the abstract of the working-paper version (CentER DP 1999-28, Tilburg) from https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/2e0fa8dd-ead5-4c6b-97cb-178d3986d48b
- **Content:** identification by restricting the signs of impulse responses, leaving the variable of interest unrestricted.
- **Use:** the reference for sign-restriction identification.

### 4.3 [P] Kilian and Murphy (2012)
- **Citation:** Kilian, L. and Murphy, D. P. (2012). "Why Agnostic Sign Restrictions Are Not Enough: Understanding the Dynamics of Oil Market VAR Models." *Journal of the European Economic Association* 10(5): 1166-1188. doi:10.1111/j.1542-4774.2012.01080.x
- **Access:** Crossref metadata; abstract from RePEc, https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jeurec/v10y2012i5p1166-1188.html
- **Quote:** "sign restrictions alone are insufficient to infer the responses of the real price of oil to such shocks." The fix is to add bounds on the short-run supply elasticity.
- **Use:** the paper's supply-constraint signature is implicitly a claim about the supply elasticity. This source says to state that bound explicitly rather than rely on signs alone.

### 4.4 [P] Baumeister and Hamilton (2019)
- **Citation:** Baumeister, C. and Hamilton, J. D. (2019). "Structural Interpretation of Vector Autoregressions with Incomplete Identification: Revisiting the Role of Oil Supply and Demand Shocks." *American Economic Review* 109(5): 1873-1910. doi:10.1257/aer.20151569
- **Access:** abstract from Crossref, matching NBER WP 24167.
- **Quote:** "Supply disruptions turn out to be a bigger factor in historical oil price movements and inventory accumulation a smaller factor than implied by earlier estimates."
- **Use:** conclusions about supply versus demand depend on priors about the elasticities, which argues for reporting results under a range of elasticity assumptions.

### 4.5 [W] Shapiro (2022), FRBSF: the closest methodological precedent
- **Citation:** Shapiro, A. H. (2022). "Decomposing Supply and Demand Driven Inflation." Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco Working Paper 2022-18, October 2022. doi:10.24148/wp2022-18. https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/wp2022-18.pdf
- **Access:** full PDF.
- **Quote:** "Shifts in demand move both prices and quantities in the same direction along the upward-sloping supply curve, while shifts in supply move prices and quantities in opposite directions along the downward-sloping demand curve. Implementing this concept empirically entails the use of a sign-restrictions (Faust (1998) and Uhlig (2005))." (§1, PDF p. 3)
- **Use:** the same idea applied to more than 100 disaggregated categories, with a simple sign rule per category and period. **Conceptual warning for the paper:** "unit values rising faster than volumes, with volumes still rising" is, under this rule, a **demand shift along a steep supply curve**, not a supply shock. The paper's hypothesis is really about supply **elasticity**, meaning capacity binding, and should be phrased that way.

### 4.6 [P] Soderbery (2015)
- **Citation:** Soderbery, A. (2015). "Estimating Import Supply and Demand Elasticities: Analysis and Implications." *Journal of International Economics* 96(1): 1-17. doi:10.1016/j.jinteco.2015.01.003
- **Access:** Crossref metadata; abstract from RePEc, https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/inecon/v96y2015i1p1-17.html , and from the SSRN version via Crossref.
- **Quote (SSRN abstract):** "Feenstra (1994) developed, and Broda and Weinstein (2006) refined, a structural estimator to estimate import demand and supply elasticities ... this paper analyzes and improves the technique to provide a unified estimator of import supply and demand elasticities."
- **Use:** the trade-specific method for estimating product-level **inverse supply elasticities** from bilateral unit values and quantities. It could turn the paper's signature into a direct estimate: a steeper supply curve for 8504.2x after 2021 than before, and than for control goods.
