Materials and trade in defence supply chains

Production shares and trade records show where materials come from. Antimony imports show how origins and unit values changed around export controls.

Evidence review · 25 November 2026 · Claim register

This note asks what production shares, import origins and historical export controls can establish about selected materials used across civilian and defence industries. It examines five material groups that illustrate mining, refining or component production with documented controls. The selection serves as a set of examples. It gives no ranking of all industrial inputs.

Finding. China-origin imports of selected antimony products fell steeply in the EU series after the 2024 controls. The EU total for the same basket held up while customs unit values rose. The separate US series shows a fall in total imports. These changes describe aggregate markets. The records give no purchase volumes or output effects for individual defence programmes. Trade exposure has a different denominator

EU magnet imports. For CN 85051110 in 2024, China accounted for 91.1% by mass (18,246,441 of 20,028,179 kg) and 92.7% by value of extra-EU imports. The code covers metal permanent magnets and magnet blanks containing specified rare earths, including neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium or samarium. Its scope differs from the IEA’s sintered magnet production series. It groups several chemistries and serves many end uses. Eurostat Comext DS-045409. Atlas magnet study.

US antimony market. USGS estimates net import reliance at 91% of apparent consumption in 2025. The numerator is imports minus exports of oxide and unwrought metal or powder. This measures the market’s reliance on imports from all sources. It gives no defence procurement share. The public USGS stockpile table lists potential transactions. Estimating coverage would require data on usable inventories and the rate of consumption. USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026, Antimony.

All five materials have a documented military use. None of these links is graded above B: official sources name the uses, and none gives the defence sector's purchases by supplier. Chinese supply inside a defence supply chain is on public record in two cases. One is antimony trisulfide, where the only qualified source was in China when the US government funded the qualification of another in fiscal 2020. The other is the magnets found on the F-35. Antimony is absent from the sourcing statute and from the NATO list, though it is in the stockpile purchase plan, which gallium and germanium are not. For gallium, germanium and tungsten no supplier relationship is public. That is an absence of evidence. It does not show that those supply chains contain no Chinese material.

The same limit applies to the buyer. The US Government Accountability Office reported in July 2025 that defence officials “cannot use FPDS to determine where components of goods that DOD procures are produced”, FPDS being the federal procurement database. For the F-35 parts they had mapped, officials had country-of-origin information “on less than 10 percent of all suppliers that provide components and raw materials”. In 2024 the same office reported, of rare earths, that the department “has not yet determined how much total domestic capacity it needs to create in the long term to meet defense-related demand”. US GAO-25-107283, Defense Industrial Base, July 2025. US GAO-24-107176, Critical Materials, September 2024.

What could replace Chinese supply

Added 25 November 2026

Three things bear on how fast a buyer could do without Chinese material: who else produces it, what can be used in its place, and what stock or new capacity exists. US law adds a constraint. The sourcing statute names four nations, China, Russia, North Korea and Iran. For the materials it covers, supply from the other three is barred as well, subject to waivers and to exceptions that include commercial off-the-shelf items and electronic devices. How the two antimony markets adjusted

The import record above is the one place on this page where an adjustment can be observed. In the EU series, imports from China fell by 182 tonnes a month between the two windows and imports from other origins rose by 237 tonnes. The total rose slightly, and the unit value of the basket rose about fourfold. In the US series imports from other origins fell as well, by 157 tonnes a month, in addition to 51 tonnes from China. That series covers a small part of the US market. It leaves out antimony oxide, which moved the other way: USGS gives US oxide imports of 24,000 tonnes in 2024 and an estimated 39,000 tonnes in 2025, while imports of unwrought metal and powder went from 4,920 to 4,500 tonnes. For the metal, the record does not show whether US buyers could not obtain material, drew on stocks or used less. Neither series separates military from civil buyers. USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026, Antimony.

What the evidence can support

Global shares show where production occurs. Import origins show where an importing market buys a customs category. Recovery is a separate question about usable supply, inventories and time. Public procurement data have limited coverage of material and lower-tier supplier origins, as documented by the US Government Accountability Office. The evidence supports the market findings above. Programme-specific exposure, stock coverage and production losses require separate evidence. US GAO report on industrial supplier visibility, July 2025.
