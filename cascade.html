How a supply shock spreads along the chain

A shock to a producer country does not stop at what it mines. Where a lost material is a host, the by-product companions recovered from it fall too: a shock to Congo’s copper is also a cobalt shock. This page follows a country’s shock through production, the companion links and trade, and scores which producer is the most systemic single point of failure.

How the cascade is computed (and where it stops)

First-order: a shock of S% to a country cuts world supply of each material it mines by S% × its production share (USGS). Companion echo: where a hit material is a host, its by-product companions lose S% × (their by-product reliance on that host), a second-order hit that the first-order view misses. Exposure: the fallout is routed to importing blocs by net trade. Each hit is expressed in absolute tonnes at risk = share of world supply lost × world production (World Mining Data 2024), where that source has a sheet for the material. The systemic score stays based on shares and rigidity on purpose: a tonnage sum would be dominated by bulk commodities, so tonnes are shown per material, not summed into the rank.

Limits: the echo only runs through hosts the atlas tracks (copper, nickel, bauxite, niobium, platinum, coking coal), so zinc/tin/zircon-hosted companions (germanium, hafnium) are under-counted for lack of their host’s production geography. First- and second-order effects only. Price feedback is tested below across a demand-elasticity band, and the top single point of failure holds. Still a structural map, not a calibrated forecast. Inputs: data.json (production) × companionality.json × net_demand.json → cascade.json.

Does it survive price feedback?

The model is linear. A real shortage would lift prices and change demand (substitution, recycling), which would soften the hit. This section tests that. The physical cascade is left unchanged (by-product tonnes are lost with their host whatever the price); a demand-response damping is applied to the impact score only, scaled by each metal’s elasticity (by-products stay inelastic), and swept across a wide band.

Where the whole arc lands

Ten layers built this up step by step: satellites cannot name a mineral → many minerals are by-products → by-products cannot scale → demand is surging and nationally concentrated. One step did not hold: the claim that a host’s cycle governs its by-products was tested and withdrawn. This page combines the others: a shock lands, passes down the companion links into metals the shocked country never mined, and then reaches the blocs that buy them. The limit is the one the atlas has stated throughout: this is a structural map from public data, not a calibrated forecast. World production tonnages now turn the shares into quantities, except for hafnium, helium, silicon, strontium, which have no World Mining Data sheet. The missing piece is real prices, to turn those quantities into values. Acquiring them is the next step, and it would turn the atlas from a demonstration into a measuring instrument.
