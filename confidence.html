What you can trust

Robustness checks on the main findings

Every number on the atlas traces to an open script. Ten automated guards run on every change, and the atlas has been through three rounds of adversarial review, which caught three real errors; all three were published. This page is the short version: what the atlas can say with confidence, and how it knows.

The discipline

One rule applies throughout: no number is hand-tuned to look right. Where the data cannot settle a question, the atlas reports the range and says so. Concentration, for instance, is shown as a spread across four reconstructions, not a single figure, because no reconstruction of customs data is ground truth.

The reliability map

What the atlas can say with confidence

The findings below rest on robust rows only. Each ends with its practical consequence.

The chokepoint is rarely the mine

Across 58 end-use supply chains, only 5 are constrained by geology — a deposit that cannot be moved. The other 53 concentrate at a stage that was built: a refinery, a fab, a separation plant, an export rule.

So what: most dependencies are not permanent. An export ban on ore cannot touch a refining chokepoint, but capital, energy policy and years of qualification can rebuild one. The stage that concentrates is the one to target, not the mine. Chokepoint map →

The seller is usually not the source

For most materials the top exporter is not the top miner. It refines or processes ore mined elsewhere and sells the product on. The gap holds when measured in tonnes as well as dollars, and on the atlas’s original materials it survives a permutation test (it is far more than chance).

So what: a customs ledger of “who exports” consistently puts the dependency in the wrong place. Trace to the refiner, and to the mine behind it, before treating a supplier as secure. The origin gap →

Concentration is moving — and we can date it

On the robust series, export concentration is rising for coking coal, manganese, bauxite, rare-earth magnets, magnesium, tungsten and strontium. Several are already single-country dominated (magnets, magnesium and tungsten toward China; bauxite toward Guinea). It is falling for copper, nickel, palladium, helium, vanadium and tantalum.

So what: the materials tightening are not always the ones in the headlines. A material that is rising and already dominated (magnesium, tungsten) is where a shock would do most damage and where diversification has the most to undo. The tested trends →

When the chokepoint is the mine, geology says why

The five geological chokepoints — phosphate rock, the platinum-group metals, boron, strontium, and niobium (the steel-alloy chain) — cannot be out-built. There is no second Bushveld for the platinum-group metals, no second Araxá for niobium.

So what: for these, the tools are substitution, recycling and stockpiling rather than new capacity elsewhere. That is a different, slower policy, and it is worth starting early. Why the mechanism matters →

What we got wrong (on purpose visible)

The atlas keeps a public ledger of its own errors. The corrections are evidence that the checking works.

  • A price-coupling claim, withdrawn. The atlas had argued that by-product metals track their host’s price; a formal test on real price series refuted the general law (only bismuth←lead survived). The supply claim stands; the price claim was retracted.
  • Cobalt, diagnosed backwards, then corrected. A first pass called a cobalt trend conflict a “thin-code artifact” on a faulty extraction. Screening thinness across every commodity up front exposed the error: cobalt is a real $0.8–4.9B market, and the finding is that the engine understated DR Congo’s real dominance. That limitation is kept in view.
  • A screen that flagged the wrong thing. An early fragility rule mislabelled nine genuinely-concentrated commodities (bauxite, magnets, tungsten) as unreliable. Fixed: single-country dominance is a finding, not a data flaw. The real fragile list is three.

The full history is in the changelog; the invitation to challenge the atlas is here.
