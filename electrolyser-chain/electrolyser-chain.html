When could iridium limit PEM electrolysis?

New capacity, catalyst loading and the share of supply available to PEM determine annual iridium requirements. Stack replacement and recycling change them over time.

Evidence review · 25 November 2026 · Claim register

Proton-exchange membrane (PEM) electrolysers usually use an iridium-based anode catalyst. The amount needed each year depends on how much new PEM capacity is built, how much iridium each stack uses and how many stacks are replaced. Alkaline, anion-exchange membrane and solid-oxide systems offer different routes for suitable applications. This study compares those choices with an explicit annual iridium budget.

Follow-up on iridium supply and new capacity

The installed base and the annual market

Global installed electrolysis capacity exceeded 4 GW in 2025 across technologies. The IEA identified more than 2.5 GW under construction targeting operation in 2026. Both refer to rated electrical input capacity. The model below uses annual additions, with a separate PEM share. The IEA identifies costs, market demand and project delays as important in the current market. IEA Global Hydrogen Review 2026, Production.

Iridium is recovered as a minor by-product of platinum-group metal mining, concentrated in South Africa. Production decisions respond to the economics of the whole mix of metals. The study uses the published 7.5 tonnes per year as a primary-output reference. Johnson Matthey separately estimates 7.1 tonnes of primary supply in 2025 on a producer-sales basis, against 7.3 tonnes of net demand. Its 3.1 tonnes of electrochemical demand cover several uses. The amount available to PEM manufacturers depends on allocation across those uses, stocks and contracts. Journal of Industrial Ecology (2026), §§2.3–2.5 and Fig. 7. Replacement and recycling change the annual balance

Modelled. The three paths are illustrative schedules for new electrolysis across all technologies from 2026 to 2050. Each starts with an empty modelled fleet. The reference assigns 50% of new capacity to PEM, keeps loading at 0.2 kg Ir per MW and allocates 30% of the primary reference to PEM (2.25 tonnes per year). That loading is below the 0.34 to 2.0 kg Ir per MW that a 2026 review reports for 2024, so the reference case is optimistic about catalyst loading. The model follows scheduled capacity even in years with a material gap. Later recycling is therefore conditional on those earlier installations going ahead.

Reducing loading, replacing PEM and recovering metal

These cases share the middle additions path. The lower-loading case reaches 0.05 kg Ir per MW in 2035. The substitution case assigns 25% of new capacity to PEM. Each case reruns the stack and recycling balance, so savings overlap. Lower loading also reduces how much iridium will return from those stacks later. A paired case halves assumed stack hours alongside lower loading to test the effect of more frequent replacement.

Existing inventories, primary expansion, refurbishment and price or procurement responses sit outside this illustration.

Published research already models iridium constraints, replacement and recycling, including feasible rates of PEM growth. This study contributes a small reproducible set of annual thresholds and tests how explicit technology and recovery choices change the balance. Estimating future shortages would require calibrated project schedules, qualified recycling volumes, stack specifications and competing demand. How much iridium is available to PEM?

Modelled. Each year, take mine-linked output, add any release of outside stocks and any recycled supply from outside the modelled fleet, and subtract other users’ net demand and any additions to outside stocks. The balance cannot fall below zero. Returns from the modelled PEM fleet enter a separate inventory. The same metal can enter the balance only once.

The example uses 7.5 tonnes a year of mine-linked output and 5.5 tonnes a year of competing net demand. Outside recycling and stock changes are set to zero. This leaves 2 tonnes a year for PEM from the market. The mine-output figure is a literature reference. The competing demand is an assumption. Johnson Matthey reports producer sales and sector net demand. Its electrochemical category covers several uses, so those totals cannot identify the amount available to PEM. Journal of Industrial Ecology (2026), §§2.3–2.5 and Fig. 7. Johnson Matthey, May 2026 PGM Market Report, Iridium supply and demand, p. 34. Johnson Matthey, Definitions of supply and demand.

How much of the middle pathway fits?

The demand calculation assumes all scheduled capacity is installed. It estimates the iridium needed and the year in which requirements after fleet recycling first exceed the market budget. The constrained case fits replacement stacks first at plants that remain open. It fits new capacity with the metal left over. Unfunded capacity waits, and future recycling comes only from stacks that entered service. Both cases start with an empty fleet. The model treats GW as divisible and carries unused recycled metal forward.

Which assumptions change the results most?

Loading has the largest effect on annual gaps over the tested ranges. The paired cases show how the result depends on stack life, how PEM share and total additions combine, how recovery and delay affect returned metal, and how competing demand changes the market budget. The ranges do not establish a ranking by cost or likelihood.
