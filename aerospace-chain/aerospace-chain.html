Where civil aero-engine supply can tighten

Titanium grade changes who can supply the material. Programme approval, available capacity and stocks determine which alternatives a buyer can use.

Evidence review · 25 November 2026 · Claim register

Abstract. We ask where supply for civil aero-engines is concentrated, which alternatives can meet the requirements of a particular programme, and what allows a buyer to keep producing during a disruption. Recalculating the European Commission Joint Research Centre’s estimates for 2023 gives a country Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI) of 4,396 for all titanium sponge capacity and 3,014 for aviation-grade capacity, a 31.4% reduction. The leading country shifts from China to Japan. Supplier approval, uncommitted capacity and stocks determine whether this different supplier geography can help a particular buyer. Public evidence supports testing these constraints for individual programmes.

The JRC estimated China’s aviation-grade capacity assuming that domestic output met 30% of Chinese aerospace demand. Its estimates cover a material category. Critical rotating parts can require a higher grade than airframes or non-rotating parts, and each buyer still needs to know which material routes are approved, deliverable and able to take additional orders. A lower country HHI alone gives no estimate of how much a buyer could obtain or how quickly. JRC Figure 5 and Annex 1.

Separate production benchmark. USGS estimates China produced 260 kt of about 370 kt of titanium sponge globally in 2025, or 70%. In its 2021–24 US sponge import series, Japan supplied 77%, Saudi Arabia 13% and Kazakhstan 8%. The import series covers sponge across grades and uses. USGS titanium summary.

What each stage can tell us

Two material routes feed the engine programme. Titanium sponge passes through melting and mill or forged products on the way to qualified titanium parts. Primary and recovered rhenium can enter nickel superalloys used in some hot-section parts. Engines bring these parts together with many other inputs. Airframe sourcing appears below as a comparator.

For a programme-specific test, identify the approved part or grade and the plant and owner supplying it in the same period. Confirm technical eligibility and the programme holder’s approval before looking at independent sources, uncommitted output, inventory and delivery timing. A country, a plant and a joint venture count different things. For engine market concentration, define the segment and the measure, such as delivered engines, then count each joint-venture programme once. Under FAA guidance, production approval holders are responsible for the conformity of their suppliers’ parts, including those from lower-tier suppliers. FAA production guidance.

Rhenium. USGS estimates 81 t of world mine-linked recovery in 2025, including 30 t in Chile (37%). The estimate for Chile includes material recovered from imported residues. Primary recovery generally accompanies copper and molybdenum production. Industry estimates cited by USGS put secondary output at 20–25 t that year, excluding recovery from catalysts reused in a closed loop. Commercial scrap recovery already supplies some new melts. These totals give no share of spare capacity or a particular programme’s supply. USGS rhenium summary.

Two documented responses

Titanium sourcing after 2022

Reported outcome. Boeing suspended titanium purchases from Russia in 2022. It reported that existing inventory and alternative sources kept the suspension from disrupting its operations that year. Its filing says qualifying alternative sources for major raw materials can take a year or more. The buyer’s stocks and alternatives explain why a loss of supply need not interrupt production immediately. The filing provides neither stock volumes nor approval dates by engine programme. Boeing 2022 Form 10-K.

Engine inspections and shop capacity

Reported outcome. The FAA required inspections and accelerated part replacements on affected commercial geared turbofan engines in 2024. The directive cited covers the PW1100G and PW1400G; other variants fall under separate directives. JetBlue reported 8 aircraft grounded for lack of engines at mid-2025, citing both required inspections and other reliability issues. It expected removed engines to take about 300 days per shop visit. The grounding count is reported and the shop duration was an estimate. This case concerns engine and maintenance availability. The disclosures give no market share or capacity estimate for a single-crystal blade caster. FAA directive. JetBlue’s June 2025 filing.

How long can inventory bridge a supply gap?

Modelled. A hypothetical civil buyer needs 100 units of one qualified input every month and loses a supplier delivering 25 units per month for 12 months. The model varies opening stock, how soon alternative deliveries begin, and how much usable supply arrives. The delays and volumes are illustrative. Boeing’s disclosure identifies the mechanisms but gives too little data to calibrate these inputs to its experience.

Customs records describe traded products

BACI counts annual traded value across borders. HS 810820 covers unwrought titanium and powders, including sponge and ingots. HS 841112 covers turbojets traded as goods within its thrust category. Neither heading identifies engine programmes, approvals, plant output or the ownership of each shipment. CEPII BACI.

What would establish a binding constraint?

The titanium comparison establishes a change in geography and concentration with grade. The cases show why usable inventory and qualified throughput matter for different outcomes. Estimating production lost or time to recover for a specific civil engine programme would require approved supplier and ownership records, available capacity by part, usable stocks and dated order or delivery data. The public series here cannot supply those inputs.
