Grid equipment in world trade, 2012–2024: did prices or volumes carry the build-out?

Critical Materials Atlas. Pre-registration, code, data and review record: buildout-study.

Abstract. Transformers, the grain-oriented electrical steel in their cores and the copper in their windings are bought by every part of the electricity build-out. Official sources record transformer lead times rising from under a year before 2020 to three years by 2024. We ask what world trade shows, using 283,029 exporter–importer–product flow-years from CEPII BACI, 2012–2024, in a design fixed before any estimate. Relative to heavy capital goods, transformer trade showed no price or volume gap in 2021–22, when producer prices rose most, and in 2023–24 both rose: unit values by +19% and tonnes by +17%. Traded supply rose with demand but at a rising price. Net of the cost of electrical steel and copper, 65% of the 2023–24 price gap remains. The comparison is a relative pattern, not a clean before-and-after: transformer unit values had been falling relative to the controls through 2012–2019, so what the data show is a reversal. Meanwhile world exports of grain-oriented electrical steel concentrated sharply: China's share rose from 14% to 36% and the three largest exporters from 55% to 72%.

1. Introduction

Public discussion of the investment wave in computing and electrification concentrates on chips. The equipment that carries electricity to them has had a harder run. Before the pandemic a large power transformer could be ordered with a lead time of under a year; by 2024, "36-month lead times" were "commonly quoted", with a maximum of 60 months[1]. Average lead times for large transformers almost doubled from 2021–22 to 2023–24 and prices rose by around 75% in real terms, according to the International Energy Agency's industry survey[6]. The US producer price index for power and distribution transformers went from 195 in 2019 to 313 in 2022 and 343 in 2024[8].

Those sources are national, or surveys. This paper asks what the world trade record shows, and asks it in a form that separates two stories. A demand boom met by expanding supply raises the value of trade through volumes. Demand meeting supply that cannot expand quickly raises it through prices. Prices and volumes rising together is demand moving along an upward-sloping supply curve[15][14], and the ratio of the two says how steep that curve was. We therefore measure how elastic traded supply was, relative to comparable goods, and how much of any transformer price rise is simply the cost of the steel and copper inside it.

Four findings. First, in 2021–22, when the US producer price index rose most, transformer trade showed no gap relative to heavy capital goods (unit value +3%, p = 0.106; tonnes +3%, p = 0.500); the cost pressure of those years hit the control goods too. Second, in 2023–24 both unit values (+19%, p = 0.011) and tonnes (+17%, p = 0.011) rose relative to the controls. Third, net of electrical-steel and copper costs, 65% of that price gap remains, so materials alone do not explain it. Fourth, the trade in grain-oriented electrical steel became far more concentrated, mostly in China. Section 7 states what none of this can show.

2. Background

Lead times and prices. The official record agrees on the direction and disagrees on magnitudes. One large US utility's lead times went from 12–18 months to 18–36 months[4]. Manufacturing a transformer with materials in hand takes only weeks; the delay is the order queue[2]. Manufacturers reported record order backlogs in 2024[6].

Cost structure. Grain-oriented electrical steel (GOES) and the copper conductor "each account for roughly 25 percent of final LPT production costs"[1][3]. GOES "prices have doubled between 2021 and mid-2023"[6]. Copper is different: "from 2021 to 2023, supply grew at a faster pace than demand, leading to a decline and stabilisation of prices"[6]. The United States has one GOES producer, which can meet 12–20% of domestic demand[1].

Evidence against a pure equipment story. US large-transformer plants raised capacity utilisation from 40% to 78% between 2011 and 2023, as reported by a national laboratory citing the US International Trade Commission[7]: domestic output did respond. And "the primary cause of delays in transmission projects remains permitting, particularly in advanced economies"[6].

3. Data

Trade flows are CEPII BACI[9], read in the HS 2002 nomenclature for every year so that no code changes under the series. A flow is one exporter–importer pair in one six-digit customs line. A flow-year enters if it is worth at least USD 100,000 and reports a positive tonnage; unit value is value over tonnes, and flow-years more than ten times above or below their line's median in that year are dropped as unit errors. The sample is 283,029 flow-years.

The controls are heavy capital goods containing steel and copper but with no grid, motor or vehicle function; electric motors, pumps and compressors share the electrification demand and appear only as a labelled robustness check. The copper price is the World Bank Pink Sheet; both it and the GOES unit value are deflated by the US consumer price index before the adjustment in design C, so that only relative input-cost movement is removed. The external benchmark is the BLS transformer producer price index[8].

4. Method

For flow f in line l and year t, in levels,

yft = β1(Tl × 2021–22) + β2(Tl × 2023–24) + αf + γt + εft,

where y is the log unit value or log tonnes, T marks the treated lines, α are flow effects and γ year effects. Holding the flow fixed removes most of the product-mix change that makes aggregate unit values unreliable[10][11][12], though not mix within a flow. Because the treatment is a shock to a product market, inference is by a wild cluster bootstrap at the level of the customs line (Webb weights, 9,999 draws, null imposed); exporter-clustered errors are reported beside it. With 9 lines, this is deliberately conservative: the smallest 2023–24 price gap the transformer design could detect with 80% power is 0.22 log points.

The design, thresholds and checks were filed before any estimate, after two independent language models reviewed a first draft as referees; the draft, the filing, three dated deviations and the code are in the study folder. One filed rule failed and is reported as such: section 6.

In 2023–24 the value of transformer trade grew, relative to the controls, through prices and volumes in about equal measure. In the language of section 1, demand rose along an upward-sloping traded supply: not a price squeeze with frozen volumes, and not a volume boom at stable prices.

5.2 A reversal, not a step

The year-by-year gaps change how the table should be read. Transformer unit values were falling relative to the controls for most of the decade, from +0.15 in 2012 to zero in 2019, and then rose to +0.26 in 2024. Relative tonnes were -0.16 in 2012, level from 2018, and +0.17 in 2024. The average-gap coefficients compare 2023–24 with a pre-period that was itself drifting, so they understate the size of the turn and overstate how clean it is.

5.3 How much is steel and copper

Taking out a quarter of the real change in the GOES unit value and a quarter of the real change in the copper price leaves a 2021–22 gap of -7% (p = 0.015) and a 2023–24 gap of +12% (p = 0.018). In the earlier period, material costs more than account for the transformer unit values; in the later one, 65% of the gap remains (a net gap of +14% with cost shares of 20% each, +11% with 30% each). By the filed rule, the 2023–24 rise is not explained by those two materials alone. Tank steel, oil and labour are not netted out; no verified source gives their shares.

5.4 The inputs

Copper wire shows no build-out signature: its premium over copper cathode moved by -4% (p = 0.023) in 2021–22 and was flat afterwards (-1%), as the IEA's account of copper supply outrunning demand would predict[6]. Grain-oriented electrical steel shows a 2023–24 unit-value gap of +12% (p = 0.074), but its placebo fails: in a fake 2015–16 post period it moves by +0.22 (p = 0.024). Its prices swing that much in ordinary years, so the trade data cannot distinguish its post-2021 movement from its normal volatility.

The price and volume of GOES trade are hard to read; its structure is not. Between 2019 and 2024 the number of exporters above 1% of world GOES exports fell from 13 to 9, the three largest went from 55% to 72% of the market, and China's share rose from 14% to 36%, as Russian exports, about a tenth of world capacity, fell under sanctions[5]. The transformer lines themselves stayed broadly supplied.

The United States and the European Union tell different stories. Into the United States, tonnes rose by +148% relative to the controls (p = 0.027) while the unit-value gap net of materials was +6% (p = 0.361): the US adjustment ran through volume, consistent with the rise in US transformer imports the national laboratories record[7]. Into the European Union both moved, prices by +28% and tonnes by +35%, and a gap of +20% remained after materials (p = 0.018).

6. Robustness, and the rule that failed

The pre-trend rule failed. The filing required every pre-period year from 2014 to 2018 to lie within ±0.05 log points of 2019 before difference-in-differences language could be used. No design met it. The results above are therefore described as relative patterns, and the reversal in section 5.2 is the honest reading.
  • Placebo period. Transformers in a fake 2015–16 post period: -0.041 (p = 0.140), consistent with the pre-2019 decline and not with a spurious rise.
  • Unit-value band. The 2023–24 price gap is +0.18 with the band at 0.2–5 times the median and +0.20 with no band.
  • Leave one line out. Dropping any single treated or control line leaves the 2023–24 price gap between +0.16 and +0.19.
  • Freight. Heavy, low-value flows, where freight costs bite: +0.13 (p = 0.036); light, high-value flows: +0.01 (p = 0.822). The gap sits in the heavier flows, but in 2023–24, after the 2021–22 freight spike had passed.
  • Exporters. The 2023–24 price gap is +0.23 for flows from China and +0.17 for all other exporters.
  • Contaminated controls. Against motors, pumps and compressors, which share the electrification demand, the gap shrinks to +0.10 (p = 0.051), as it should if those goods rose too.
  • Are unit values prices? The annual change in the unit value of US transformer imports correlates with the change in the BLS producer price index at only 0.43 (levels: 0.53). Unit values carry product mix as well as price, and every price result here inherits that weakness.

7. What this cannot say

  • Nothing about AI or data centres specifically. Every buyer of grid equipment is in these flows: utilities, renewable developers, factories, vehicle makers.
  • Nothing about domestic production. A country that builds its own transformers never appears in trade. US plants' rising utilisation is outside these data.
  • Unit values are not prices. Within a six-digit line, a shift towards larger or more efficient transformers raises unit value without any price change; the price gaps are upper bounds.
  • Not causal, and silent on why. The data show that traded supply rose at a rising price; they do not show whether factories, electrical steel, skilled labour or permitting was the constraint, and the IEA names permitting as the main cause of transmission delays in advanced economies[6].
  • Chip-making equipment is not tested. Its unit values per tonne measure which machines were shipped. Its trade grew from $0bn in 2012 to $0bn in 2024; the largest exporters in 2024 were .

8. Conclusion

World trade in transformers carried the electricity build-out through both prices and volumes, and only from 2023: traded supply expanded, but at a rising price, after a decade in which transformers had been getting cheaper relative to comparable capital goods. The cost of steel and copper explains the 2021–22 movement and 35% of the later one. The part of the chain that became genuinely more concentrated is the steel inside the transformer, where one country now supplies over a third of world exports and the three largest over two-thirds. For a buyer, the practical reading is that transformer supply did respond to demand, but through the order book and the price; for a policy maker, the concentration in electrical steel, not in transformers, is the exposure the trade data can see.
