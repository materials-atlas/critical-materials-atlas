The AI build-out in customs data

When people ask what a data centre needs, the answer is usually chips. This page asks something customs data can actually answer: over the last five years, which of the things a data centre is made of grew fastest in world trade? Deflated by US consumer prices, trade in transformers grew 71–117% between 2019 and 2024 while trade in integrated circuits grew 13–39%, and electric motors, pumps and compressors, which the same electrification buys, 1–67%. Growth in trade value is not a test. Our research note tests transformers flow by flow, 2023–24 against their 2012–2020 average: against its filed comparison of heavy machinery, transformer unit values stood +19% and tonnes +17% higher, but against motors, pumps and compressors the gaps (+10% and +9%) cannot be told apart from zero. The rise may belong to electrification generally, not to transformers, let alone to data centres. What stands out more clearly is one step upstream: China is now the largest exporter of the steel inside transformers.

Three things this table does not say. It does not say the AI build-out caused any of this: transformers and electrical steel are bought by every kind of electrification, from grid replacement to electric vehicles, and the data cannot separate a data centre's transformer from a substation's. The four lines under “other electrical machinery” are the goods the research note uses as a robustness comparison, because they share that demand. It does not measure production or installation, only what crossed a border. And a customs line is not a product: the notes under each line say where the code is wider than its name.

What the newer months add, line by line and without attributing any of it to AI:

  • Memory chips. In January–July 2026 the value of memory imports was up 199% on the same months of 2025 in the EU and up 217% in the US. These are values, so the records alone cannot say how much of that is price and how much is quantity.
  • Large transformers kept rising. EU imports of the largest units (over 10,000 kVA) rose 58% in 2025 and 32% in January–July 2026, and China supplied 55% of them in 2025. US imports of the two larger classes rose 47% and 38% in 2025, with China at 7% and 2%: the two markets buy from different places.
  • Electrical steel. China supplied 57% of the EU's imports of the wide line in 2025 and 0.5% of the US's, the same split the research note traces. Imports are not supply: counting what EU mills make, China was about a quarter of the EU's electrical steel and a tenth of its transformers in 2024 (see the note, section 4.6b).
  • Neon, in the US record. The US record splits the rare-gas line at ten digits (the EU's splits out helium but not neon). In 2025 neon, the gas used in chip lithography, was 0.8% of the value of US rare-gas imports, and helium 90%. The line’s value says almost nothing about neon.
  • Photosensitive devices and LEDs, mostly solar cells and modules: US imports fell 42% in 2025.

Lines made of a few large shipments, such as chip-making machines, swing widely from one period to the next; a single year's change there is not a trend.

The world panel tells the same story as the two national records: memory up 224% in the first half of 2026 (on a panel holding 34% of that line's world imports), transformers up in 2025, and China's share of the electrical-steel lines higher again. Values are in current dollars, so a rise mixes price and quantity.

What “building your own fab” buys

A company that decides to make its own chips does not escape the supply chain; it moves to the part of it that sells the factory. The machines that make integrated circuits were a $77bn line in 2024, up 52% in constant dollars since 2019, and its three largest exporters — the Netherlands, Japan, Singapore — ship 65% of it. The customs line that carries photoresists (along with other photographic chemicals) is narrower still: one country, Japan, ships 57% of it. None of this says anything about any particular company’s chip, its cost or its schedule, which customs data cannot see. It says who a new fab would be buying from.

Which of these actually move with the chip cycle?

A second question, and a harder one: when world trade in integrated circuits rises, does trade in each input rise with it? The table gives the elasticity from twenty-two annual observations (2003–2024), and beside it the smallest elasticity that many observations could have detected. Read the last column first: for most of these lines the detectable size is larger than any plausible answer, so the estimate carries no information. Both sides are in current dollars; the same inflation sits on both, but a common price cycle can still move them together without any link between the two industries.

No line here is readable, including the one with a small p-value. Seven lines were tested with no correction for testing seven, and a common price cycle can move both sides together. Rare gases comes in at +0.39% per 1% of chip trade with p = 0.007, close to the smallest effect twenty-two observations could detect; and that line is mostly helium, which follows industry at large. Treat this table as a list of things to measure properly with monthly data, not as a result.

Which lines are worth watching

Two things are true at once, and only one of them is about concentration. The transformer lines are small and growing fast, but they are less concentrated than the chip lines, not more: their largest exporters hold 13–23% of world exports against 21–38% for processors and memory. What is both small and becoming more concentrated is grain-oriented electrical steel, the core of a transformer. Taking its two customs lines together (read in the 2002 nomenclature, as in the note), world exports were $4.0bn in 2024; China's share of them rose from 12% in 2019 to 32%, and the three largest exporters' from 48% to 67%, partly replacing Russian exports. The research note follows this to July 2026: in the EU's imports the shift to China continued, while direct US imports of the steel still come mostly from Japan and South Korea. That makes it a material to watch, which is not the same as a demonstrated constraint — nothing on this page measures whether anyone was actually short of it.
