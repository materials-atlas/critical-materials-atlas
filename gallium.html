Deep dive · one metal, followed to the bottom

Gallium, end to end

Every other page compares metals. This one follows one metal physically, from the bauxite it occurs in to the chip it ends up in, and puts numbers, with bounds, on the claim the atlas rests on: that a by-product can’t scale to its own price. For gallium the claim can be demonstrated, not only asserted.

The mass balance, and every constant it rests on

World bauxite production (World Mining Data 2024) × the gallium content of bauxite (~50 ppm, literature range 30–80) gives the gallium embedded in mined ore. Multiplying by the share of bauxite processed to alumina (~85%) gives the gallium passing through alumina refineries, the only place it is economically recoverable. This is compared with actual primary gallium production (WMD ~987 t; other estimates 300–760 t, because China, ~99% of supply, does not report). The gap is what is discarded.

Bias bounds: the ppm and production figures are the uncertain terms; every step below carries its low–high bracket. Gallium production statistics are contested, and that uncertainty is part of the finding. Trade caveat: gallium exports under HS 811292, shared with germanium (identical unit values). 811292 is a customs catch-all also nominally covering hafnium/indium/niobium/rhenium/vanadium, so “gallium trade” is a bundle (see below). Inputs: production.json + reconciled trade → gallium.json.

Why price can’t fix it

The gallium exists: about 17,596 tonnes of it flow through alumina refineries every year. What is missing is recovery capacity. The extraction step is built at only a handful of plants, and adding it means retrofitting a refinery, a multi-year capital project. So when gallium’s price quadrupled after China’s 2023 export controls, world supply could not follow. There was no idle capacity to switch on, and the raw material’s economics are set by aluminium, not gallium. The atlas draws this distinction throughout, between a metal that can be mined in greater quantity and one that can only be recovered in greater quantity, and slowly. Gallium is the clearest example: a supply chain that discards most of the gallium in its feedstock by design, capped upstream by a host industry it does not control.

Comparison with published work. Published work agrees, and the comparison is used as a check. Peer-reviewed gallium material-flow studies put Bayer-liquor losses at roughly 95–96% and find that ~90% of primary gallium comes from that one Bayer route. This atlas reaches ~94% discarded from bauxite tonnage and recovery bounds, close to the same figure. This is a consistency check, not an independent measurement: the atlas calculation uses the same load-bearing constants as the material-flow studies (gallium ppm in bauxite, Bayer-liquor recovery). The agreement rules out an arithmetic or bookkeeping error in the atlas, but it does not independently confirm the shared assumption. The atlas draws the same line elsewhere for USGS/WMD (“independent compilations, not independent measurements”); it applies here too. The one constant everything rests on (ppm) is shown with its range. One caveat: gallium can be recovered at much higher rates from new manufacturing scrap (offcuts from making GaAs wafers, with recovery near 27%). That looks like an escape valve, but it is not a second mine. New scrap is a fixed fraction of gallium already produced and sold, so it scales with demand, not against a shortage. It cannot add new primary supply; recycling is covered on its own page.

Why one metal, done deep, matters

A scorecard across 32 materials shows breadth; a single chain followed to the bottom shows the mechanism is real. Gallium, traced physically, turns every abstract axis of the hardest-cases scorecard into a number that can be checked: can’t scale becomes “94% discarded, capped by refinery retrofits”; concentrated becomes “99% from one country that doesn’t report”; thin market becomes “under 1,000 tonnes a year”; trade illusion becomes “bundled with germanium under one catch-all HS code.” Here the atlas’s thesis rests on one metal’s physical flows, with the error bars drawn in, rather than on a correlation across a spreadsheet.
