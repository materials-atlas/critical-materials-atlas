Platinum-group metals · Research note

Platinum, palladium and rhodium have different supply maps

South Africa supplies most platinum and rhodium, and Russia leads in palladium. Recycling and vehicle demand also differ by metal, so one figure for the group hides the exposure.

Evidence review · 25 November 2026 · Claim register

Platinum, palladium and rhodium are mined together, refined together and used mainly in vehicle exhaust catalysts. They are often treated as one material. This note sets out what public sources show for each metal separately: where it is mined, how much comes back from recycling, how much of its demand is automotive, and what the customs records can and cannot show.

Finding. In 2025 South Africa supplied 71% of primary platinum and 81% of primary rhodium, but 34% of primary palladium, where Russia led with 45%. Vehicles took 42% of platinum demand, 81% of palladium demand and 85% of rhodium demand. All three markets drew down stocks in 2025. A single figure for the group hides these differences. Johnson Matthey, PGM Market Report, May 2026.

Mine production differs by metal

USGS publishes mine production for platinum and palladium by country, and reserves for the group as a whole. South Africa mined 70% of the world's platinum in 2024 and Russia 12%. For palladium the order is reversed: Russia 41%, South Africa 38%. Zimbabwe, Canada and the United States supplied most of the rest. USGS gives no mine production by country for rhodium. USGS, 2026 edition.

Between the 2025 and 2026 editions USGS cut its Russian reserve figure from 16,000 to 11,000 tonnes and its world figure from “more than 81,000” to “more than 76,000” tonnes. It also revised 2024 mine production upward for both leading producers: palladium from an estimated 190 to 217 tonnes worldwide, and platinum from 170 to 179 tonnes. USGS, 2025 edition. Supply and demand in 2025, metal by metal

Johnson Matthey publishes a yearly balance for each metal. Its supply figures are producer sales, allocated to the region where the metal was mined, so they differ from USGS mine output. Its recycling figures count only metal that returns to the open market. Metal that an industrial user recycles and keeps is netted out of demand instead. Johnson Matthey, PGM Market Report, May 2026.

All three markets were in deficit in 2025: demand exceeded supply and stocks fell. The automotive share of demand is the main difference between the metals. Platinum has large jewellery, industrial and investment uses. Palladium and rhodium depend on vehicles for more than four-fifths of demand. Johnson Matthey, PGM Market Report, May 2026.

What recycling adds, and when

Recycling supplied 22% of platinum, 32% of palladium and 31% of rhodium reaching the market in 2025. Most of it is scrap from vehicles: 73% of recycled platinum, 85% of recycled palladium and all of the recycled rhodium that Johnson Matthey reports. Johnson Matthey, PGM Market Report, May 2026.

Metal inside vehicles on the road is not a stock that can be drawn on in a supply interruption. It returns when vehicles are scrapped, which the industry association puts at 15 to 18 years after sale, and only where converters are collected and processed. Johnson Matthey reports that recycling in Europe, North America and Japan was unusually weak in 2023 and 2024 because owners kept vehicles longer. The amount recycled in a year therefore follows past vehicle sales and scrappage, not the current price or a current shortage. International Platinum Group Metals Association. Johnson Matthey, PGM Market Report, May 2026.

Two screens of exposure

Modelled. The first screen removes half of one country's primary sales for a year and holds everything else at its 2025 value. It gives the lost metal as a share of 2025 demand, before any use of stocks or extra recycling. It carries no probability.

A South African interruption weighs most on platinum and rhodium. A Russian interruption weighs most on palladium. For comparison, the 2025 deficits were 11.4% of demand for platinum, 4.1% for palladium and 4.7% for rhodium.

Modelled. The second screen lowers automotive demand and holds supply and all other demand at their 2025 values. It shows the resulting market balance. Battery-electric vehicles need no exhaust catalyst, so this is the direction of the long-run change, but the size and timing of any fall are not estimated here.

With automotive demand a quarter lower, palladium and rhodium would move into surplus and platinum would stay close to balance. The screen leaves out two effects that work in the other direction. The three metals come out of the same ore, so a mine that cut output because palladium was in surplus would also produce less platinum and rhodium. And vehicle scrap is most of the recycled supply, so fewer combustion vehicles would later mean less recycled metal. Neither effect is measured here. IPA rhodium fact sheet. USGS Professional Paper 1802-N.

What the customs records cover

The trade table uses four customs codes. Three cover unwrought metal or powder, one each for platinum, palladium and rhodium. The fourth, 711510, covers catalysts in the form of wire cloth or grill of any platinum-group metal, which are industrial gauzes. Vehicle catalytic converters are not in any of the four. Before 2022 they were classified with other gas-filtering equipment under 842139, and a separate code, 842132, exists only from the 2022 nomenclature. Catalysts on a support are classified under 3815. The table therefore shows trade in refined metal, much of it passing through refining and trading hubs, and says nothing about where converters are made. WCO, HS 2017, Chapter 71. Tonnage check. The extract records 3,923 tonnes of palladium trade in 2002 and 5,254 tonnes in 2009, against 334 to 509 tonnes in the other years. Six bilateral records explain the difference: five imports into Canada in 2002 and one shipment from the United Kingdom to Cyprus in 2009. Each carries a weight about a thousand times what its value implies, at around one US cent per gram when palladium traded at several dollars per gram. Without them the totals are 442 tonnes for 2002 and 406 tonnes for 2009. This page uses trade values only. The tonnage series should not be used for those two years. CEPII BACI V202601.

What remains open

Mine shares, recycling shares and automotive shares are measured. Three questions that matter for supply security are not. The first is how mine economics link the metals: whether lower palladium demand would cut platinum and rhodium output. The second is refining capacity by country, which the public sources do not give. The third is how recycled supply will change as the vehicle fleet changes. Rhodium, iridium and ruthenium have no published mine production by country in the USGS series, so their supply geography rests on producer-sales estimates.

Revision note · 25 November 2026

The previous overview treated the three metals as one material. It said that two countries were “essentially the only” mine sources, that recycled metal supplied “a quarter or more” of supply and cushioned supply shocks as a hidden reserve, and that the real threat was a collapse in vehicle demand. This revision gives each metal's figures, corrects the mislabelled history chart, replaces the reserve-cushion claim with what the recycling figures measure, and replaces the demand claim with a labelled screen. The claim register lists each change.
